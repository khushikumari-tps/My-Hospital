The Programme

The one cancer you can prevent by looking

The large intestine is the final stretch of the digestive tract — roughly a metre and a half running from the caecum, up the right side, across the abdomen, down the left, and into the rectum. It reclaims water and salt from what the small intestine has finished with, and stores the residue until it leaves.

Most disease here is benign: polyps, diverticula, haemorrhoids, inflammation. Much of it can be diagnosed and treated during a single colonoscopy. What makes this organ unusual is its cancer — almost all colorectal cancer begins as an adenomatous polyp that takes years to turn malignant. That long window is why screening works better here than almost anywhere else in medicine: removing the polyp prevents the cancer outright rather than catching it early.

Advitya's colorectal programme brings gastrointestinal surgeons, gastroenterologists, radiologists, oncologists, stoma nurses and dietitians around a single table — so screening is timely, staging is precise, and sphincter preservation is considered in every case where the tumour allows it.

Conditions We Treat

The full spectrum of colorectal disease

From a polyp snared at screening to a total mesorectal excision — each condition is managed by the same specialist team, using one shared treatment plan.

Colon Polyps

Growths on the bowel lining that accumulate genetic changes over years. Adenomatous polyps are the origin of the great majority of colorectal cancers — and removing them at colonoscopy interrupts that sequence entirely.

Preventable

Diverticular Disease

Small pouches herniating through the colon wall, associated with low dietary fibre. Usually silent, but they can inflame, bleed or perforate — and recurrent complicated attacks are an indication for resection.

Common

Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease — chronic immune-mediated inflammation managed medically first, with surgery held in reserve. Cancer risk rises with duration and extent, so surveillance matters.

Long-term care

Colon Cancer

Malignancy of the caecum, ascending, transverse, descending or sigmoid colon. Often silent until it bleeds or obstructs, which is why iron-deficiency anaemia in an adult is investigated rather than simply treated.

Surgical oncology

Rectal Cancer

Staged separately from colon cancer, because pelvic MRI and the mesorectal margin decide whether chemoradiation precedes surgery and whether the sphincter can be preserved.

Multidisciplinary

Benign Anorectal Disease

Haemorrhoids, fissures, fistulae and perianal abscess. Common, treatable, and worth examining properly — assuming piles without looking is the commonest route to a late cancer diagnosis.

Day care

Hereditary Syndromes

Lynch syndrome and familial adenomatous polyposis carry very high lifetime risk. They need genetic counselling, family screening and intensive surveillance beginning in early adulthood rather than at 45.

Warning Signs

Six symptoms worth a specialist opinion

Individually these are common and often harmless. Together, or persisting beyond a couple of weeks, they deserve a colonoscopy rather than reassurance.

Change in bowel habit

New constipation, looser stools or alternating between the two, lasting weeks.

Rectal bleeding

Fresh blood, or darker blood mixed into the stool — never assume it is piles.

Unexplained anaemia

Iron deficiency in an adult implies slow blood loss from the gut until proven otherwise.

Abdominal pain and bloating

Colicky pain with distension, which can signal a partially obstructing lesion.

Incomplete evacuation

A persistent urge that passing stool does not relieve — typical of a rectal mass.

Unintentional weight loss

Weight loss alongside any of the above raises concern considerably.

Treatment & Surgery

Every option, under one team

Endoscopic, laparoscopic and open approaches, alongside the medical and nutritional care that determines how well you recover.

Polypectomy & EMR

Removal of polyps during colonoscopy, including endoscopic mucosal resection for larger flat lesions. No incision, no admission.

Laparoscopic Colectomy

Keyhole removal of the affected segment for cancer, diverticular disease or polyps that cannot be snared, with faster recovery than open surgery.

Total Mesorectal Excision

The precision operation for rectal cancer — removing the rectum within its intact fascial envelope, preserving the sphincter wherever the tumour allows.

Total Colectomy

Removal of the entire colon for severe inflammatory bowel disease or familial polyposis, with pouch reconstruction where appropriate.

Chemotherapy

Adjuvant treatment after surgery for node-positive disease, and neoadjuvant chemoradiation before surgery for locally advanced rectal cancer.

IBD Medical Therapy

Targeted anti-inflammatory and biologic therapy for ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease, with structured monitoring for response and relapse.

Anorectal Procedures

Day-care treatment for haemorrhoids, fissures and fistulae, using stapled, laser and conventional techniques as the condition dictates.

Stoma Care & Nutrition

Specialist stoma nursing before and after surgery, with dietetic support through the graded return to a normal diet.

Frequently Asked

Questions patients ask us most

When should I have my first colonoscopy?

At 45 if you are at average risk. If a first-degree relative has had colorectal cancer, begin ten years before the age at which they were diagnosed. Symptoms at any age — bleeding, a persistent change in bowel habit, unexplained anaemia — mean colonoscopy now rather than at a screening milestone.

Is rectal bleeding always serious?

No. Haemorrhoids and anal fissures are far commoner causes than cancer. But the two cannot be distinguished reliably by symptoms alone, and assuming the benign explanation without examination is the single commonest reason colorectal cancer is diagnosed late. Bleeding warrants assessment, not reassurance over the phone.

Is a colonoscopy uncomfortable?

It is performed under sedation and most patients remember little of it. The bowel preparation the day before is the part people find least pleasant, and modern low-volume preparations are considerably better tolerated than older regimens. The procedure itself usually takes twenty to thirty minutes.

Will I need a stoma after colorectal surgery?

In most colon operations, no — the bowel is rejoined during the same procedure. For rectal cancer a temporary stoma is sometimes created to protect the join while it heals, and is usually reversed after a few months. A permanent stoma is needed only when the tumour involves the anal sphincter itself, and we will tell you before surgery which situation applies to you.

How is diverticular disease treated?

Most episodes of uncomplicated diverticulitis settle with antibiotics and dietary modification. Surgery is reserved for:

  • Recurrent attacks that disrupt daily life
  • Complications — perforation, abscess, fistula or stricture
  • Bleeding that cannot be controlled endoscopically

A high-fibre diet after recovery reduces the chance of further episodes.

How long is recovery after colorectal surgery?

Expect three to seven days in hospital after major surgery, less after a laparoscopic procedure. Light activity resumes within a few weeks and full recovery typically takes four to six weeks. Diet returns gradually, starting low-fibre or soft, guided by our dietetic team.

Can colorectal cancer be prevented?

To a considerable extent, yes — more so than most cancers. Regular colonoscopy detects and removes the polyps that would otherwise become cancer. Alongside that, a diet high in whole grains, fruit and vegetables, maintaining a healthy weight, regular exercise, and avoiding smoking and heavy alcohol each reduce risk independently.

Do you review reports before I travel for an appointment?

Yes. For outstation patients, a video review of your existing scans, colonoscopy report and biopsy is usually the first step, so the trip is made only when it will change something. Bring the actual films or CD rather than only the printed report, along with previous prescriptions, discharge summaries and blood work from the last three months.
