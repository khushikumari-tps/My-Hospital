Know the signs

Symptoms of Stomach Cancer

Early symptoms are often mild and easy to mistake for ordinary indigestion. Symptoms that persist for more than a few weeks should be evaluated.

01

Persistent Indigestion

Heartburn, burning or indigestion that keeps returning or does not settle with usual remedies.

02

Abdominal Discomfort or Pain

A vague ache, pressure or pain in the upper abdomen, often felt after eating.

03

Loss of Appetite

Not feeling hungry, or losing interest in food, without an obvious reason.

04

Unexplained Weight Loss

Losing weight without changing your diet or activity levels.

05

Nausea or Vomiting

Feeling sick, or vomiting, particularly after meals or on a regular basis.

06

Difficulty Swallowing

Food feeling stuck, or discomfort when swallowing, especially with solid foods.

07

Feeling Full Quickly

Feeling full or bloated after eating only a small amount of food.

08

Fatigue

Ongoing tiredness or weakness, which may be related to anaemia from slow blood loss.

09

Blood in Vomit or Stool

Vomiting blood, or passing black, tarry stools, needs urgent medical evaluation.

Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Vomiting blood, black or tarry stools, severe abdominal pain, inability to swallow or keep fluids down, or sudden weakness require urgent medical assessment.

Patient education

What is Stomach Cancer?

Stomach cancer, also called gastric cancer, develops when abnormal cells begin to grow in the lining of the stomach and form a tumour.

How it develops

The stomach is lined by a layer of cells that produce acid and digestive juices. When some of these cells change and start to multiply in an uncontrolled way, they can form a growth in the stomach wall. Over time this growth may spread deeper into the wall, to nearby lymph nodes, or to other organs.

Where it appears

Stomach cancer can start in any part of the stomach — near the junction with the food pipe, in the main body of the stomach, or near the outlet into the small intestine. The location affects the symptoms and the type of surgery that may be needed.

Why symptoms are often late

Early stomach cancer frequently causes few or no symptoms, or symptoms that feel like ordinary indigestion. This is why persistent digestive symptoms, especially in adults over 40 or with risk factors, should be evaluated rather than ignored.

Why evaluation matters

Whether surgery is required, and how much of the stomach needs to be removed, depends on the stage of the cancer, its location and your overall health. Endoscopy, biopsy and scans allow the team to plan treatment properly.

Diagnosis and treatment should be based on endoscopy, biopsy and staging investigations, reviewed by a specialist team.

Understanding the causes

Risk Factors for Stomach Cancer

Having a risk factor does not mean you will develop stomach cancer, and some people develop it without any known risk factor. These are the factors most often associated with the disease.

Helicobacter Pylori Infection

Long-standing infection with this stomach bacterium is one of the most important known risk factors.

Family History

A close relative with stomach cancer, or certain inherited conditions, increases risk.

Smoking

Tobacco use raises the risk of cancer in the stomach, particularly near the food pipe.

Certain Dietary Factors

Diets high in salted, smoked or preserved foods and low in fresh fruit and vegetables.

Chronic Stomach Inflammation

Long-term gastritis and changes in the stomach lining can increase the risk over time.

Previous Stomach Conditions

Earlier stomach surgery, stomach polyps or pernicious anaemia are associated with higher risk.

Increasing Age

Stomach cancer becomes more common with age and is most often diagnosed in people over 50.

Discuss Your Risk

If several of these apply to you, or symptoms persist, an evaluation with a specialist is advised.

Evaluation & staging

How is Stomach Cancer Diagnosed?

Diagnosis confirms whether cancer is present; staging shows how far it has spread. Both are needed before treatment can be planned.

01

Clinical Evaluation

Your symptoms, medical history, risk factors and previous reports are reviewed, and you are examined.

02

Upper GI Endoscopy

A thin flexible camera is passed through the mouth to examine the lining of the food pipe and stomach directly.

03

Biopsy

Small tissue samples taken during endoscopy are examined under a microscope to confirm the diagnosis and type of cancer.

04

CT Scan

A CT scan of the chest, abdomen and pelvis shows the extent of the tumour and whether nearby organs or lymph nodes are involved.

05

PET/CT When Appropriate

In selected cases a PET/CT scan may be used to look for spread that is not visible on other scans.

06

Blood Tests

Blood tests check for anaemia, nutrition, organ function and general fitness for treatment.

07

Additional Staging Investigations

Endoscopic ultrasound, diagnostic laparoscopy or other tests may be advised to complete staging before treatment.

08

Fitness Assessment

Heart, lung and nutritional status are assessed so that treatment can be planned safely.

09

Multidisciplinary Review

Results are discussed by surgeons, oncologists, radiologists and pathologists to agree a treatment plan.

The stage and type of stomach cancer can only be confirmed by endoscopy, biopsy and appropriate imaging.

Treatment

Stomach Cancer Treatment

Treatment depends on the cancer’s stage, its location in the stomach and your overall health. Most patients receive a combination of treatments planned by a multidisciplinary team.

Surgery

Surgery to remove the tumour, part or all of the stomach and nearby lymph nodes is the main treatment for many stomach cancers that have not spread to distant organs.

Surgical options

Chemotherapy

Medicines that destroy cancer cells. Chemotherapy is often given before surgery to shrink the tumour, after surgery to reduce the risk of recurrence, or as the main treatment when surgery is not suitable.

Discuss with our team

Radiation Therapy

High-energy rays directed at the tumour. Used in selected situations, sometimes combined with chemotherapy, or to relieve symptoms such as bleeding or pain.

Discuss with our team

Targeted Therapy

Medicines that act on specific features of cancer cells, such as the HER2 protein. Whether these are suitable depends on tests carried out on the biopsy tissue.

Discuss with our team

Immunotherapy

Treatments that help the body’s own immune system recognise and attack cancer cells. Offered to selected patients based on the stage of the cancer and test results.

Discuss with our team

Multidisciplinary Cancer Care

Surgeons, medical and radiation oncologists, radiologists, pathologists and dietitians review each case together so that the sequence of treatments is planned for the individual patient.

Discuss with our team

No single treatment is right for everyone. The recommended plan is decided after staging and multidisciplinary review.

Surgical care

Stomach Cancer Surgery

The operation is chosen according to where the tumour is, how far it has spread and your general health. The digestive tract is reconnected so that you can continue to eat.

Partial Gastrectomy

Removal of the affected portion of the stomach, most often the lower part, together with nearby lymph nodes. The remaining stomach is joined to the small intestine. Considered when the tumour is confined to one region of the stomach.

Discuss with our team

Total Gastrectomy

Removal of the entire stomach when required, for example when the cancer is in the upper stomach or involves a large area. The food pipe is joined directly to the small intestine. Eating patterns change, and dietary support is part of recovery.

Discuss with our team

Lymph Node Surgery

Removal and examination of the lymph nodes around the stomach as part of the operation. This is an important part of cancer treatment and staging, helping to determine whether further treatment is needed after surgery.

Discuss with our team

The type and extent of surgery can only be decided after endoscopy, biopsy, staging scans and multidisciplinary review.

Surgical approach

Minimally Invasive Stomach Cancer Surgery

Laparoscopic or robotic approaches may be appropriate for selected patients depending on the cancer and the clinical situation. Not every patient is eligible, and the choice of approach is a clinical decision.

Approach 01

Laparoscopic Surgery

The operation is carried out through several small incisions using a camera and long, fine instruments. The same cancer operation — removal of the tumour, part or all of the stomach and the lymph nodes — is performed, viewed on a screen.

  • Small incisions rather than one large cut.
  • May be considered for suitable early-stage tumours in selected patients.
  • Suitability depends on tumour stage and location, previous surgery and anaesthetic fitness.
Approach 02

Robotic-Assisted Surgery

A form of minimally invasive surgery in which the surgeon controls robotic instruments from a console. The instruments provide a magnified view and fine movement. It is used for selected patients where the facility and clinical situation allow.

  • Magnified three-dimensional view and precise instrument movement.
  • Not suitable for every tumour or every patient.
  • Open surgery remains appropriate for many patients and may be safer in some situations.

The appropriate surgical approach is decided after staging and clinical evaluation, and may change during surgery if required for safety.
