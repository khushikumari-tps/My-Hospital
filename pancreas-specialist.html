Focus

Specialist expertise in pancreatic disease

The pancreas sits deep in the abdomen, behind the stomach, sharing its blood supply with the bowel and the bile duct. That position is why pancreatic problems are often found late, and why the operations that treat them are among the most demanding in abdominal surgery.

A specialist practice matters here for a plain reason: these conditions are uncommon, the decisions are finely balanced, and the difference between an operation that helps and one that does not is usually made before the first incision — in the reading of the scans and the assessment of the patient.

Consultations cover the whole path: what the imaging shows, whether an operation is appropriate at all, what the alternatives are, and what recovery would involve.

Surgical expertise

Pancreatic operations performed

Which operation applies depends on the part of the gland involved and on your assessment. Not every pancreatic tumour can be removed, and an honest answer about that is part of the first consultation.

Whipple procedure

Pancreaticoduodenectomy, for tumours of the head of the pancreas. The head of the gland, the duodenum, the gallbladder and the lower bile duct are removed together, and the digestive tract reconstructed.

How the Whipple procedure works →

Distal pancreatectomy

For tumours of the body and tail of the gland, with or without removal of the spleen depending on what the tumour involves.

Total pancreatectomy

Where the whole gland is involved. It carries lifelong consequences for digestion and blood sugar, so it is planned carefully and discussed in full beforehand.

Vascular resection and reconstruction

Where a tumour abuts the portal or superior mesenteric vein. This is the subject of Dr. Kapoor’s published research on resections performed alongside pancreatectomy.

Minimally invasive and robotic approaches

In selected patients, decided on the tumour, the anatomy and your fitness for the approach — not applied as a default.

Surgery for benign disease

Operations for non-cancerous pancreatic tumours and cysts, and for chronic pancreatitis where pain or duct obstruction is not settling with other treatment.

Conditions

Pancreatic conditions seen in this clinic

If you have been told you have one of these, or a scan has raised the question, a consultation starts with reading the reports you already have.

Pancreatic cancer

Cancer arising in the head, body or tail of the gland. Jaundice, weight loss, upper abdominal or back pain, and new diabetes in later life are among the reasons people are referred.

Pancreatic cancer surgery →

Acute pancreatitis

Sudden inflammation of the gland, most often from gallstones or alcohol. Severe cases need hospital care, and the cause has to be treated so it does not recur.

Understanding acute pancreatitis →

Chronic pancreatitis

Long-standing inflammation that changes the gland permanently, bringing pain, poor digestion and sometimes diabetes. Treatment aims at pain, nutrition and any blocked duct.

Understanding chronic pancreatitis →

Pancreatic cysts and benign tumours

Some are harmless and only need watching; some carry a risk of change over time. Which is which is decided on the imaging and, where needed, on further tests.

Pancreatic disease overview →

Bile duct and gallbladder disease

The bile duct passes through the head of the pancreas, so obstruction, stones and tumours there are assessed by the same specialty.

Why consult a specialist

What this practice brings to a pancreatic problem

Specialist pancreatic care

Pancreatic disease is the focus of the practice rather than one item on a general surgical list.

Multidisciplinary evaluation

Imaging, pathology and, where relevant, oncology input are brought together before a treatment plan is settled.

Individualised treatment planning

The plan follows your scans, your fitness and your circumstances. No package is applied in advance of the assessment.

Care through the whole episode

Pre-operative assessment, the operation itself, and the follow-up and rehabilitation that come after it.
