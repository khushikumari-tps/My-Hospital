Biliary Disease

Understanding benign biliary conditions

The biliary system carries bile from the liver to the intestine. Most problems with it are non-cancerous, but they still cause real discomfort and can lead to serious complications if left alone.

What are benign biliary diseases?

These are non-cancerous conditions affecting the bile ducts or gallbladder. The common ones are:

  • Cholelithiasis (gallstones): stones formed in the gallbladder or bile ducts.
  • Cholangitis: inflammation of the bile ducts, usually from infection or obstruction.
  • Choledocholithiasis: gallstones lodged in the common bile duct.
  • Biliary strictures: narrowing of the bile ducts, often after injury or inflammation.
  • Biliary dyskinesia: dysfunctional bile duct or gallbladder motility.
  • Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC): a chronic autoimmune disease causing bile duct damage.

What causes them?

  • Gallstones: bile composition imbalance, obesity, or rapid weight loss.
  • Cholangitis: usually bacterial infection following bile duct obstruction.
  • Strictures: may develop after surgery, injury or chronic inflammation.
  • Biliary dyskinesia: often idiopathic, though stress and hormonal change may contribute.
  • Primary biliary cholangitis: an autoimmune reaction damaging the small bile ducts.

What are the symptoms?

  • Right upper abdominal pain, especially after fatty food.
  • Jaundice — yellowing of the skin and eyes.
  • Dark urine and pale stools.
  • Nausea and vomiting.
  • Fever and chills, suggesting infection.
  • Persistent fatigue, common in PBC.

How are they diagnosed?

  • Medical history and physical examination.
  • Blood tests: liver function tests, bilirubin, and markers such as ALP and GGT.
  • Ultrasound: first-line for gallstones and bile duct obstruction.
  • MRCP: non-invasive magnetic resonance imaging of the bile ducts.
  • ERCP: used for both diagnosis and treatment.
  • Biopsy: may be required for conditions such as primary biliary cholangitis.

What are the treatment options?

Treatment follows the specific condition rather than the symptom.

  • Gallstones: asymptomatic stones may need no treatment; cholecystectomy is recommended for symptomatic stones.
  • Cholangitis: antibiotics plus bile duct drainage via ERCP.
  • Biliary strictures: endoscopic stenting or balloon dilation to open the narrowed duct.
  • Biliary dyskinesia: cholecystectomy if symptoms are severe.
  • Primary biliary cholangitis: medication such as ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) to slow progression.

What complications can arise if untreated?

  • Biliary colic: severe pain from gallstone obstruction.
  • Acute cholangitis: a life-threatening infection of the bile ducts.
  • Pancreatitis: inflammation of the pancreas from bile duct obstruction.
  • Biliary cirrhosis: chronic liver damage from prolonged obstruction or disease.

When should you consult a specialist?

  • Persistent abdominal pain or discomfort.
  • Symptoms of jaundice or infection — fever, chills.
  • Recurrent episodes of biliary colic.
  • Diagnosed gallstones with complications such as pancreatitis or cholangitis.

Are they preventable?

  • Maintain a healthy weight — obesity increases gallstone risk.
  • Adopt a balanced diet, high in fibre and low in unhealthy fats.
  • Stay hydrated to support bile production and flow.
  • Exercise regularly.
  • Treat infections or gallstone symptoms early, before complications develop.
