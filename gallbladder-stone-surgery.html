The condition

Understanding gallstones

The gallbladder is a small sac tucked under the liver. It stores bile, the fluid the liver makes to help digest fat, and squeezes it into the intestine after a meal. Gallstones are solid pieces that form in that stored bile, ranging from grains of sand to a stone several centimetres across.

Many people carry gallstones for years without knowing. Trouble starts when a stone blocks the gallbladder outlet — typically after a fatty meal — producing pain under the right ribs that can last minutes to hours.

Stones can also move out of the gallbladder into the bile duct, where they may block the drainage of bile from the liver or the pancreas. That is why jaundice, fever or severe upper abdominal pain in someone with known gallstones is treated urgently.

Symptoms

When should you seek medical evaluation?

Gallstone symptoms are often mistaken for indigestion. These patterns are worth having assessed properly.

Pain after fatty meals

Pain under the right ribs or in the upper abdomen, often starting within an hour or two of eating.

Pain into the back or shoulder

Discomfort that travels to the right shoulder blade or through to the back.

Nausea and vomiting

Feeling sick during an episode of pain, sometimes with bloating.

Yellowing of eyes or skin

Jaundice, dark urine or pale stools suggest the bile duct may be involved — seek care promptly.

Fever with pain

Fever or chills alongside upper abdominal pain needs same-day medical attention.

Repeated episodes

Attacks that keep coming back, even if each one settles on its own.

Diagnosis

How the diagnosis is reached

Ultrasound answers most of the question. Liver function tests and an MRCP are added when the bile duct may be involved.

Which tests apply to your case is a clinical decision made after you have been examined and your existing reports reviewed.

STEP 01

Consultation

The pattern of pain, its relation to meals and any previous episodes are discussed.

STEP 02

Clinical examination

The abdomen is examined, along with a check for jaundice.

STEP 03

Ultrasound

An abdominal ultrasound is the usual first test for gallstones and shows the gallbladder wall and the ducts.

STEP 04

Blood tests and further imaging

Liver function tests, and where the duct may be involved, an MRCP or CT scan.

STEP 05

Treatment planning

Findings are put together with your symptoms to decide whether surgery is advised, and when.

Treatment

Treatment options

Stones that have never caused symptoms are often left alone and simply monitored. Once stones have caused pain or a complication, they tend to cause trouble again, so removal of the gallbladder is commonly recommended.

Medicines do not reliably dissolve gallstones, and stones left in place after an attack can lead to inflammation of the gallbladder, blockage of the bile duct or inflammation of the pancreas.

If a stone has already passed into the bile duct, it may need to be cleared endoscopically by ERCP before or around the time of gallbladder surgery.

What the recommendation depends on

  • Condition
  • Severity
  • Diagnosis
  • Imaging
  • Patient factors
  • Overall clinical assessment

No procedure is promised in advance of an assessment. What is appropriate for you is decided after examination and a review of your investigations.

Surgical approach

Laparoscopic gallbladder surgery

Removing the gallbladder — cholecystectomy — is most often done laparoscopically, through several small incisions using a camera. Digestion continues normally afterwards: bile simply flows from the liver into the intestine without being stored first.

A laparoscopic operation is occasionally converted to an open one during surgery when the anatomy is unclear or inflammation is severe. That is a safety decision, not a complication, and it is discussed with you beforehand as a possibility.

Procedures offered

  • Laparoscopic cholecystectomy for symptomatic gallstones
  • Management of acute cholecystitis, with timing decided on your presentation
  • Assessment and clearance of bile duct stones, including ERCP where indicated
  • Open cholecystectomy where laparoscopic surgery is not appropriate
  • Evaluation of gallbladder polyps and thickened gallbladder walls

FAQs

Questions people ask before booking

Do gallstones always need surgery?

No. Stones found by chance that have never caused symptoms are often just monitored. Surgery is usually discussed once stones have caused pain, inflammation or a blockage.

Can gallstones be dissolved with medicine?

Medication works only in a narrow set of circumstances, takes a long time and stones frequently return. For most people with symptoms it is not a practical alternative to surgery.

Can I digest food normally without a gallbladder?

Yes. The gallbladder stores bile rather than making it. After removal, bile flows continuously from the liver into the intestine. Some people notice looser stools for a few weeks; this usually settles.

Is the operation done by keyhole surgery?

Most gallbladder removals are laparoscopic. Whether that is suitable for you depends on inflammation, previous abdominal surgery and your general health.

What happens if a stone is stuck in the bile duct?

It usually needs to be removed, commonly by an endoscopic procedure called ERCP, in addition to gallbladder surgery. The order and timing depend on how you present.

How soon can I return to work?

That depends on the type of surgery and the nature of your work. Your team will give you specific advice on activity, driving and lifting before discharge.

What should I bring to my consultation?

Your ultrasound or CT report and images, recent blood tests including liver function tests, a list of your medicines, and dates of any previous episodes of pain.
