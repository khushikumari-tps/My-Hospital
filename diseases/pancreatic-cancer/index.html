Overview

Understanding Pancreatic Cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma arises from the cells lining the pancreatic duct. It is difficult to detect early because the pancreas sits deep in the abdomen and the tumour produces no symptoms until it presses on something — most often the bile duct, which is why painless jaundice is the classic presentation of a tumour in the head of the gland.

Everything turns on staging. A dedicated pancreatic-protocol CT defines the tumour's relationship to the surrounding arteries and veins, and that anatomy determines whether the disease is resectable, borderline resectable or locally advanced. These are not degrees of severity but distinct treatment pathways, and getting the classification right is the most consequential decision in the entire pathway.

Surgery offers the only realistic prospect of cure, but it is one part of a multimodal plan. Chemotherapy before surgery can convert borderline disease to resectable and treats micrometastases that imaging cannot see. Every case should be discussed in a joint surgical and oncology tumour board rather than decided by any single clinician.

Symptoms

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

None of these confirms a diagnosis on its own. Each is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see how things develop.

Painless Jaundice

Progressive yellowing of the eyes and skin without pain, with dark urine and pale stools — the most important single presenting sign.

Unexplained Weight Loss

Substantial, unintended weight loss over weeks to months, frequently the earliest change patients recall in retrospect.

Epigastric or Back Pain

A deep, gnawing pain in the upper abdomen radiating to the back, often worse lying flat and eased by curling forward.

New-Onset Diabetes

Diabetes appearing after fifty with no family history, particularly alongside weight loss, is an established warning sign.

Persistent Itching

Bile salts deposited in the skin causing an intense generalised itch that often precedes visible jaundice.

Pale, Greasy Stools

Obstruction of the pancreatic duct preventing enzymes reaching the intestine, so fat passes through undigested.

Causes & Risk

Causes and Risk Factors

Understanding what drives the condition is what makes prevention and early detection practical rather than theoretical.

Causes

  1. Acquired Genetic MutationsSequential mutations in KRAS, CDKN2A, TP53 and SMAD4 accumulating in duct cells and driving progression from precursor lesion to invasive cancer.
  2. Chronic Pancreatic InflammationLong-standing chronic pancreatitis creating a persistently inflamed environment in which malignant change becomes considerably more likely.
  3. Precursor Cystic LesionsIntraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms and mucinous cystic neoplasms, which carry a defined and manageable risk of transformation.
  4. Inherited Cancer SyndromesBRCA2, Lynch syndrome, Peutz-Jeghers and hereditary pancreatitis, which together account for roughly one in ten cases.

Risk Factors

  1. SmokingThe strongest modifiable risk factor, roughly doubling risk. Risk falls steadily after cessation but takes many years to approach that of a non-smoker.
  2. Age and Family HistoryIncidence rises sharply after sixty. Two or more affected first-degree relatives define familial pancreatic cancer and justify formal surveillance.
  3. Long-Standing DiabetesDiabetes of more than five years' duration modestly raises risk, while new diabetes may itself be an early manifestation of the tumour.
  4. Obesity and Chronic PancreatitisBoth create sustained inflammatory and metabolic conditions that favour malignant transformation over time.
Diagnosis

How We Reach a Diagnosis

Only the investigations that will change a decision are ordered, and they are sequenced so results build on one another rather than repeating.

Pancreatic-Protocol CT

The cornerstone of staging. Timed arterial and venous phases define the tumour's relationship to the vessels and determine resectability.

Endoscopic Ultrasound with FNA

Detects small tumours CT cannot resolve and provides tissue diagnosis before any chemotherapy is started.

MRI and MRCP

Characterises liver lesions of uncertain nature and maps duct anatomy where the CT appearance is equivocal.

CA 19-9 and Liver Function

A baseline tumour marker for monitoring response, interpreted alongside bilirubin since obstruction alone raises it.

Staging Laparoscopy

Detects small peritoneal or liver deposits invisible on imaging, sparing selected patients a major operation that could not have helped them.

Treatment

Treatment Options

Treatment is matched to the stage and to the individual. Not every option below applies to every patient, and the right choice is made together.

Surgical

Whipple's Procedure

Pancreaticoduodenectomy for tumours of the head — removal of the pancreatic head, duodenum, bile duct and gallbladder, with reconstruction of all three streams.

Surgical

Distal Pancreatectomy

Resection of the body and tail with splenectomy, performed laparoscopically in suitable cases with faster recovery than open surgery.

Oncological

Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy

Given before surgery to treat micrometastatic disease and to downstage borderline resectable tumours into operable ones.

Supportive

Biliary Stenting and Symptom Control

Endoscopic stenting to relieve jaundice, enzyme replacement for malabsorption, and specialist pain management including coeliac plexus block.
