Overview

Understanding Biliary Disease

The biliary tree is the plumbing between liver, gallbladder, pancreas and intestine. Bile made in the liver drains through progressively larger ducts into the common bile duct, joins the pancreatic duct, and enters the duodenum. It is a low-pressure system, and it tolerates obstruction badly.

When flow is blocked — by a stone, a stricture or a tumour — bile backs up. Bilirubin spills into the blood and the eyes yellow, urine darkens, stools pale, and the skin itches. If bacteria colonise the stagnant bile above the blockage, cholangitis follows: fever, rigors and jaundice together, which is a medical emergency rather than an outpatient problem.

Bile duct injury deserves separate mention. It is an uncommon but serious complication of gallbladder surgery, and it is one of the few conditions where the outcome depends less on the injury itself than on who repairs it and when. Reconstruction is specialist work, and results are markedly better when it is done once, correctly, in a unit that performs it regularly.

Symptoms

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

None of these confirms a diagnosis on its own. Each is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see how things develop.

Progressive Jaundice

Yellowing of the eyes and skin that deepens over days to weeks, usually painless when the cause is a stricture or tumour rather than a stone.

Dark Urine, Pale Stool

Bile pigment diverted into the urine darkens it, while its absence from the intestine leaves stools putty-coloured — a specific and often overlooked pair of signs.

Persistent Itching

Bile salts deposited in the skin cause an unrelenting itch that worsens at night and does not respond to ordinary antihistamines.

Fever with Rigors

Shaking chills with jaundice and right upper abdominal pain form Charcot's triad and indicate acute cholangitis — an emergency needing urgent drainage.

Right Upper Abdominal Pain

Colicky pain when a stone is moving through the duct; a duller, more constant ache when the obstruction is fixed.

Unexplained Weight Loss

Steady weight loss with painless jaundice raises the possibility of a malignant rather than benign cause and warrants prompt imaging.

Causes & Risk

Causes and Risk Factors

Understanding what drives the condition is what makes prevention and early detection practical rather than theoretical.

Causes

  1. Common Bile Duct StonesStones formed in the gallbladder migrate into the duct, or form there directly after previous surgery, obstructing flow and seeding infection.
  2. Benign StricturesScarring narrows the duct, most often following gallbladder surgery, chronic pancreatitis or repeated episodes of inflammation.
  3. Post-Surgical Bile Duct InjuryDamage during cholecystectomy, ranging from a minor leak to complete transection, requiring specialist assessment and planned reconstruction.
  4. Autoimmune and Inflammatory DiseasePrimary sclerosing cholangitis and IgG4-related disease inflame and progressively narrow the ducts without any mechanical cause.

Risk Factors

  1. Previous Gallbladder SurgeryRetained or newly formed duct stones and post-operative strictures are the commonest reasons for biliary problems after cholecystectomy.
  2. Chronic PancreatitisA fibrotic pancreatic head compresses the lower common bile duct, producing a smooth, tapering stricture and slow-onset jaundice.
  3. Recurrent CholangitisEach episode of infection scars the duct a little further, creating a cycle in which strictures beget more infection.
  4. Inflammatory Bowel DiseaseUlcerative colitis carries a well-recognised association with primary sclerosing cholangitis and warrants a low threshold for liver function testing.
Diagnosis

How We Reach a Diagnosis

Only the investigations that will change a decision are ordered, and they are sequenced so results build on one another rather than repeating.

Liver Function Tests

An obstructive pattern — raised alkaline phosphatase and GGT with bilirubin — points to the biliary tree rather than the liver itself.

Ultrasound

Establishes whether the ducts are dilated, which distinguishes obstruction from liver disease and directs everything that follows.

MRCP

The reference non-invasive study. It maps the entire biliary tree, showing the level and nature of the blockage before any instrument is passed.

Endoscopic Ultrasound

Detects small duct stones and early tumours that MRCP can miss, and allows tissue sampling in the same sitting.

ERCP

Both diagnostic and therapeutic — stones are extracted, strictures brushed for cytology and stents placed to restore drainage.

Treatment

Treatment Options

Treatment is matched to the stage and to the individual. Not every option below applies to every patient, and the right choice is made together.

Endoscopic

ERCP with Sphincterotomy

Duct stones are removed and drainage restored endoscopically, avoiding open surgery in the great majority of cases.

Endoscopic

Biliary Stenting and Dilation

Balloon dilation with temporary stenting reshapes benign strictures over months, with a graded programme of stent exchanges.

Reconstructive

Hepaticojejunostomy

The definitive repair for bile duct injury and complex strictures — the duct is joined directly to a Roux limb of jejunum, bypassing the damaged segment.

Medical

Cholangitis Management

Prompt antibiotics with urgent biliary decompression. In acute cholangitis, drainage is the treatment and antibiotics alone are not enough.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers to the questions patients ask most often in clinic.

Yellowing of the eyes and skin, dark urine, pale stools and persistent itching are the classic four. Pain may or may not be present — its absence is not reassuring, since painless jaundice is more often the pattern with strictures and tumours. Fever and shaking chills alongside jaundice indicate infection and require emergency care.

Blood tests first establish an obstructive pattern, then ultrasound determines whether the ducts are dilated. MRCP maps the biliary tree in detail without instrumentation and is the reference study. ERCP is reserved for when treatment is intended, since it carries its own small risk and is no longer used for diagnosis alone.

It is performed under sedation or general anaesthesia, so it is not painful at the time. Mild throat discomfort and bloating for a day afterwards are common. The main risk is post-ERCP pancreatitis, which occurs in a small minority and is minimised by careful technique and appropriate patient selection.

Poorly performed repair leads to recurrent strictures, persistent bile leaks, repeated cholangitis and, over years, secondary biliary cirrhosis. Success rates in specialist high-volume units are substantially better than in centres that see these injuries rarely, which is why referral matters more here than in almost any other biliary condition.

Any new jaundice in an adult should be assessed within days, not weeks. Fever with jaundice needs same-day emergency care. Persistent itching, pale stools, or abnormal liver function tests found on routine screening also warrant specialist review even when you feel well.

Many can. Benign strictures often respond to a planned programme of endoscopic balloon dilation and stenting over six to twelve months. Surgery is reserved for strictures that fail to respond, injuries involving loss of duct continuity, and cases where malignancy cannot be excluded.
