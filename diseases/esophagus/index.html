Overview

Understanding Esophageal Disease

The oesophagus is a muscular tube that moves food from throat to stomach by coordinated waves of contraction. A valve at its lower end opens to let food through and closes to keep acid out. Almost all oesophageal disease is a failure of one of those two functions — the muscle or the valve.

When the valve fails, acid refluxes upward and causes heartburn. Persistent reflux over years can change the lining of the lower oesophagus into an intestinal-type epithelium, a condition called Barrett's oesophagus. Barrett's is not cancer, but it is the recognised precursor to adenocarcinoma, which is why it is kept under surveillance.

When the muscle fails, swallowing itself becomes difficult. Achalasia — where the lower valve does not relax — produces a slow, progressive obstruction that patients often adapt to for years before seeking help. Any difficulty swallowing that is getting worse, particularly progressing from solids to liquids, needs an endoscopy promptly.

Symptoms

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

None of these confirms a diagnosis on its own. Each is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see how things develop.

Persistent Heartburn

A burning sensation rising behind the breastbone, typically worse lying flat, after large meals or on bending forward.

Difficulty Swallowing

A sensation of food sticking. Progression from solids to liquids over months is the pattern that most demands prompt investigation.

Regurgitation

Undigested food or sour fluid returning to the mouth without nausea, particularly at night, sometimes staining the pillow.

Chronic Cough or Hoarseness

Reflux reaching the larynx causing a persistent dry cough, throat clearing or voice change, often mistaken for a respiratory problem.

Non-Cardiac Chest Pain

Squeezing central chest pain from oesophageal spasm that can closely mimic cardiac pain and must be distinguished from it first.

Weight Loss with Dysphagia

Losing weight because swallowing has become difficult is a red-flag combination requiring urgent endoscopy.

Causes & Risk

Causes and Risk Factors

Understanding what drives the condition is what makes prevention and early detection practical rather than theoretical.

Causes

  1. Lower Sphincter IncompetenceA weak or inappropriately relaxing valve at the gastro-oesophageal junction allowing acid and bile to reflux upward.
  2. Hiatal HerniaPart of the stomach sliding through the diaphragm into the chest, disrupting the anatomy that normally keeps the valve competent.
  3. Achalasia and Motility DisordersDegeneration of the nerve plexus in the oesophageal wall, so peristalsis fails and the lower sphincter does not relax.
  4. Peptic Stricture and Barrett's ChangeYears of acid exposure producing fibrotic narrowing, or metaplastic transformation of the lining into intestinal-type epithelium.

Risk Factors

  1. Obesity and Central AdiposityRaised intra-abdominal pressure is the strongest modifiable risk factor for reflux and for hiatal hernia formation.
  2. Smoking and AlcoholBoth reduce sphincter tone and impair mucosal healing. They are also the principal risk factors for squamous cell carcinoma of the oesophagus.
  3. Long-Standing RefluxThe duration of reflux, more than its severity, determines the risk of developing Barrett's oesophagus and its subsequent complications.
  4. Dietary and Postural HabitsLarge late meals, caffeine, and lying down soon after eating each provoke reflux and are among the easiest factors to change.
Diagnosis

How We Reach a Diagnosis

Only the investigations that will change a decision are ordered, and they are sequenced so results build on one another rather than repeating.

Upper GI Endoscopy

Direct visualisation of the lining to identify oesophagitis, stricture, hernia or Barrett's change, with biopsy where indicated.

Esophageal Manometry

Measures the pressure and coordination of the muscular contractions, and is the definitive test for achalasia and motility disorders.

24-Hour pH Monitoring

Quantifies actual acid exposure over a full day, distinguishing true reflux disease from symptoms with another cause.

Barium Swallow

A dynamic study showing the shape of the oesophagus and how a bolus travels, particularly useful for strictures and large hernias.

Endoscopic Ultrasound

Assesses depth of invasion when a tumour is found, which determines whether endoscopic resection is adequate.

Treatment

Treatment Options

Treatment is matched to the stage and to the individual. Not every option below applies to every patient, and the right choice is made together.

Conservative

Lifestyle and Dietary Therapy

Weight reduction, smaller earlier meals, head-of-bed elevation and trigger avoidance, which control symptoms in a substantial proportion of patients.

Medical

Acid Suppression

Proton pump inhibitors to heal oesophagitis and control symptoms, reviewed periodically rather than continued indefinitely without reassessment.

Endoscopic

Dilation and Ablation

Balloon dilation for strictures and achalasia, and radiofrequency ablation for dysplastic Barrett's segments, both performed without incisions.

Minimally Invasive

Laparoscopic Fundoplication and Heller Myotomy

Keyhole reconstruction of the valve for refractory reflux, or division of the lower sphincter muscle for achalasia, with a partial wrap to prevent reflux.
