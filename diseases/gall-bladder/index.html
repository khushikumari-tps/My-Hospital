Overview

Understanding Gall Bladder Disease

The gallbladder is a small, pear-shaped reservoir tucked beneath the liver. It concentrates the bile the liver produces and releases it into the intestine when a meal containing fat arrives. It is a storage organ, not a manufacturing one — which is why life continues comfortably after it is removed.

Problems begin when bile falls out of balance and forms stones, or when the outlet becomes blocked. A stone that lodges in the cystic duct causes biliary colic: a hard, gripping pain under the right ribs that builds over an hour and often follows a rich meal. If the blockage persists, the wall becomes inflamed and infected, and the picture turns from painful to urgent.

Not every gallstone needs an operation. Stones found by chance, causing no symptoms, are usually watched. Stones that have caused pain tend to cause it again, and in that setting removal of the gallbladder is the definitive answer — done laparoscopically, through four small incisions, with most patients home the same day.

Symptoms

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

None of these confirms a diagnosis on its own. Each is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see how things develop.

Biliary Colic

Severe, gripping pain in the upper right abdomen that builds over an hour and often radiates to the right shoulder blade, classically after a fatty meal.

Nausea and Vomiting

Sickness accompanying the pain, sometimes with retching that brings no relief until the stone shifts back out of the duct.

Fever and Chills

A rising temperature with the pain suggests the gallbladder wall is inflamed or infected, and needs same-day assessment rather than a wait-and-see approach.

Jaundice

Yellowing of the eyes or skin with dark urine and pale stool means a stone has moved into the bile duct and is obstructing bile flow.

Fatty Food Intolerance

Bloating, belching and discomfort after oily or fried food, often dismissed as ordinary indigestion for months before the diagnosis is made.

Recurrent Right-Sided Discomfort

A dull, recurring ache below the ribs between attacks, typically worse in the evening and easy to attribute to something else.

Causes & Risk

Causes and Risk Factors

Understanding what drives the condition is what makes prevention and early detection practical rather than theoretical.

Causes

  1. Bile Composition ImbalanceWhen bile carries more cholesterol than its bile salts can keep dissolved, crystals form and grow into stones over months to years.
  2. Poor Gallbladder EmptyingA gallbladder that contracts weakly leaves bile standing, and standing bile thickens into sludge that seeds stone formation.
  3. Cystic Duct ObstructionA stone wedged in the outlet traps bile under pressure, which produces the pain of biliary colic and, if it stays, acute cholecystitis.
  4. Chronic InflammationRepeated low-grade irritation thickens the gallbladder wall, producing adenomyomatosis and, over many years, a small increase in cancer risk.

Risk Factors

  1. Female Sex and PregnancyOestrogen raises cholesterol saturation in bile and progesterone slows gallbladder emptying, which is why gallstones are considerably more common in women.
  2. Obesity and Rapid Weight LossBoth extremes matter. Excess weight raises cholesterol secretion; crash dieting and bariatric surgery cause rapid stone formation.
  3. Age Over FortyStone prevalence climbs steadily with each decade, and the proportion of patients presenting with complications rises alongside it.
  4. Family History and DiabetesA first-degree relative with gallstones roughly doubles risk, and diabetes contributes through altered motility and lipid handling.
Diagnosis

How We Reach a Diagnosis

Only the investigations that will change a decision are ordered, and they are sequenced so results build on one another rather than repeating.

Blood Tests

Liver function tests, bilirubin and inflammatory markers separate simple colic from cholecystitis or duct obstruction.

Ultrasound

The first-line study. It shows stones, wall thickening and duct dilatation with high accuracy and no radiation.

CT Scan

Reserved for complications — perforation, abscess or an unclear acute abdomen — rather than for finding stones.

MRCP

Non-invasive magnetic resonance imaging of the bile ducts, used when a stone is suspected to have passed into the common bile duct.

HIDA Scan

A functional study measuring how well the gallbladder empties, used when pain is typical but ultrasound shows no stones.

Treatment

Treatment Options

Treatment is matched to the stage and to the individual. Not every option below applies to every patient, and the right choice is made together.

Conservative

Medical Management

Analgesia, antibiotics where infection is present, and dietary modification stabilise an acute attack and buy time for planned surgery.

Endoscopic

ERCP with Stone Extraction

When a stone has migrated into the common bile duct, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography clears it and relieves the obstruction before surgery.

Minimally Invasive

Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy

The definitive treatment. Four keyhole incisions, a critical-view dissection to protect the bile duct, and same-day discharge for most patients.

Complex

Open Cholecystectomy

Chosen deliberately where dense adhesions, severe inflammation or unclear anatomy make keyhole dissection unsafe. Conversion is a judgement, not a failure.
