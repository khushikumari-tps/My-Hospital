Overview

Understanding Gall Bladder Cancer

Gallbladder cancer is uncommon worldwide but strikingly more frequent across northern India and the Gangetic plain, where it is among the leading gastrointestinal cancers in women. It arises from the gallbladder lining, most often after years of chronic inflammation from gallstones.

Its defining clinical problem is that it hides behind a common benign disease. The symptoms — right upper abdominal pain, nausea, fatty food intolerance — are indistinguishable from ordinary gallstone disease. As a result a significant proportion is discovered incidentally, by the pathologist, in a gallbladder removed for what everyone believed were simple stones.

That incidental diagnosis is not a failure; it is a recognised pathway with a defined response. It requires prompt restaging, review of the original operative findings and histology, and in most cases a planned completion operation — a liver bed resection with regional lymphadenectomy — performed at a specialist centre without delay.

Symptoms

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

None of these confirms a diagnosis on its own. Each is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see how things develop.

Persistent Right Upper Pain

A constant ache under the right ribs that differs from the intermittent, self-limiting pattern of ordinary biliary colic.

Jaundice

Yellowing of the eyes and skin, indicating that the tumour has involved the bile duct or the hilum of the liver.

Unintentional Weight Loss

Steady weight loss with abdominal symptoms, which distinguishes malignancy from benign gallstone disease more reliably than pain does.

Palpable Right-Sided Mass

A firm mass felt below the right costal margin, a late sign indicating locally advanced disease.

Loss of Appetite

Persistent early satiety and reduced appetite, frequently attributed to indigestion for months before investigation.

Ongoing Nausea

Nausea that does not settle between episodes, in contrast to the discrete attacks typical of gallstone colic.

Causes & Risk

Causes and Risk Factors

Understanding what drives the condition is what makes prevention and early detection practical rather than theoretical.

Causes

  1. Chronic Gallstone InflammationLong-standing stones causing repeated injury to the gallbladder lining, with cellular DNA damage accumulating over years.
  2. Porcelain GallbladderCalcification of the gallbladder wall following prolonged inflammation, a finding that raises cancer risk enough to justify removal.
  3. Gallbladder PolypsPolyps exceeding one centimetre, or those growing on serial imaging, carry a meaningful risk of harbouring malignancy.
  4. Anomalous Pancreaticobiliary JunctionA congenital variant allowing pancreatic enzymes to reflux into the gallbladder, producing chronic chemical irritation of the lining.

Risk Factors

  1. Geographic and Ethnic FactorsIncidence in northern India, and among Indigenous populations of the Americas, is many times that of Western Europe.
  2. Female Sex and Age Over SixtyWomen are affected two to three times more often than men, largely reflecting the higher prevalence of gallstone disease.
  3. Large or Long-Standing StonesStones over three centimetres, and stone disease present for decades, both correlate with substantially higher risk.
  4. Chronic Typhoid CarriagePersistent Salmonella typhi carriage in the gallbladder is a recognised risk factor of particular relevance in the Indian subcontinent.
Diagnosis

How We Reach a Diagnosis

Only the investigations that will change a decision are ordered, and they are sequenced so results build on one another rather than repeating.

High-Resolution Ultrasound

Often the first study to raise suspicion — focal wall thickening, a polypoid mass or loss of the normal wall layering.

Contrast-Enhanced CT

Defines local invasion into the liver bed, involvement of the hepatic vessels, nodal spread and distant metastases.

MRI with MRCP

Clarifies extension into the bile ducts and the liver hilum, which is what determines the extent of resection required.

PET-CT

Used selectively to detect occult distant disease before committing a patient to major surgery, particularly after incidental diagnosis.

Histopathology Review

Re-examination of the original specimen after incidental diagnosis, since T-stage and margin status determine what completion surgery is needed.

Treatment

Treatment Options

Treatment is matched to the stage and to the individual. Not every option below applies to every patient, and the right choice is made together.

Surgical

Radical Cholecystectomy

Removal of the gallbladder with a wedge of adjacent liver segments IVb and V, together with a systematic regional lymphadenectomy.

Surgical

Completion Surgery

Definitive re-operation after incidental diagnosis, including port-site consideration and liver bed resection, planned promptly once restaging is complete.

Complex

Extended Hepatectomy with Bile Duct Resection

For tumours involving the hilum or right portal structures, planned on volumetry to ensure adequate liver remnant.

Oncological

Adjuvant Chemotherapy

Systemic treatment after resection to address microscopic residual disease, with palliative regimens and biliary stenting where cure is not achievable.
