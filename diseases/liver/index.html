Overview

Understanding Liver Disease

The liver is the body's chemical plant. It processes everything absorbed from the gut, manufactures bile and clotting factors, stores energy, and clears drugs and toxins. It also has a property no other solid organ shares: it regenerates. Remove a substantial portion and the remainder grows to compensate within weeks.

That regenerative capacity is what makes major liver surgery possible, and it shapes how every operation is planned. Before a resection we measure the volume of liver that will remain and assess how well it functions. The question is never simply whether the lesion can be removed — it is whether enough healthy liver will be left behind to carry the patient through.

Many liver findings need no treatment at all. Simple cysts and haemangiomas are discovered by chance on scans done for other reasons and are entirely harmless. The clinical skill lies in distinguishing these confidently from the lesions that do require action, which is done on imaging characteristics rather than size alone.

Symptoms

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

None of these confirms a diagnosis on its own. Each is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see how things develop.

Persistent Fatigue

Disproportionate tiredness that rest does not resolve, often the earliest and most easily dismissed sign of impaired liver function.

Right Upper Abdominal Discomfort

A dull ache or dragging sensation under the right ribs, typical of a stretched liver capsule from an enlarging lesion or fatty infiltration.

Jaundice

Yellowing of the eyes and skin indicating that the liver is failing to clear bilirubin, or that the biliary drainage is obstructed.

Abdominal Swelling

Fluid accumulating in the abdomen, a sign of advanced disease with portal hypertension that requires prompt specialist assessment.

Itching Without Rash

Bile salts in the skin producing a generalised itch, characteristically worse at night and unresponsive to ordinary treatments.

Unintentional Weight Loss

Steady weight loss with reduced appetite, warranting imaging to exclude a malignant cause.

Causes & Risk

Causes and Risk Factors

Understanding what drives the condition is what makes prevention and early detection practical rather than theoretical.

Causes

  1. Metabolic Fatty Liver DiseaseFat accumulating within liver cells in association with obesity, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes — now the commonest liver condition worldwide.
  2. Chronic Viral HepatitisLong-standing hepatitis B or C infection driving continuous inflammation, fibrosis and, over decades, cirrhosis.
  3. Alcohol-Related Liver InjurySustained heavy intake causing fatty change, then hepatitis, then irreversible scarring, with considerable variation between individuals.
  4. Benign Structural LesionsSimple cysts, haemangiomas and adenomas arising without any underlying liver disease, most found incidentally during imaging.

Risk Factors

  1. Obesity and Type 2 DiabetesThe central drivers of metabolic fatty liver disease, and the reason liver disease is rising in populations where alcohol intake is low.
  2. Hepatitis B or C StatusChronic infection is the single strongest risk factor for cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, and both are detectable by a simple blood test.
  3. Long-Term Alcohol UseRisk rises with cumulative dose over years. Women develop injury at lower intakes than men for reasons that are not fully understood.
  4. Oral Contraceptive and Steroid UseLong-term use is associated with hepatic adenoma, a benign tumour that can bleed and occasionally transform, and which needs monitoring.
Diagnosis

How We Reach a Diagnosis

Only the investigations that will change a decision are ordered, and they are sequenced so results build on one another rather than repeating.

Liver Function Tests

Transaminases, alkaline phosphatase, albumin and clotting together indicate both the pattern of injury and the reserve remaining.

Ultrasound with Elastography

Detects fat, lesions and duct dilatation, and measures liver stiffness as a non-invasive surrogate for fibrosis.

Triple-Phase CT

Timed contrast imaging characterises lesions by their blood supply and provides the volumetry that surgical planning depends on.

MRI with Contrast

The most accurate way to characterise indeterminate lesions and to distinguish benign from malignant without biopsy.

Targeted Biopsy

Reserved for lesions that imaging cannot resolve, since biopsy carries a small risk of bleeding and of seeding along the needle track.

Treatment

Treatment Options

Treatment is matched to the stage and to the individual. Not every option below applies to every patient, and the right choice is made together.

Conservative

Lifestyle and Metabolic Therapy

Structured weight reduction, glycaemic control and alcohol cessation, which can reverse fatty liver disease and early fibrosis entirely.

Surveillance

Structured Monitoring

Interval imaging for benign lesions and six-monthly surveillance for patients with cirrhosis, where early detection changes what treatment is possible.

Minimally Invasive

Laparoscopic Liver Resection

Keyhole removal of peripheral lesions, with less pain, shorter stay and faster return to work than open surgery in suitable cases.

Complex

Major Hepatectomy

Anatomical resection of a lobe or extended segments, planned on volumetry so that sufficient functional liver remains after surgery.

Up to about seventy percent of a healthy liver can be resected, because the remnant regenerates within weeks. In a liver damaged by cirrhosis or chemotherapy the safe margin is considerably smaller, which is why we measure remnant volume and function before deciding rather than during surgery.

Liver surgery is major surgery, and the liver's rich blood supply means bleeding is the principal concern. In specialist units with careful preoperative planning, modern parenchymal transection techniques and enhanced recovery protocols, outcomes have improved substantially over the past two decades.

Most patients stay in hospital five to ten days after a major resection, and two to four days after a laparoscopic one. Light activity resumes within two to three weeks and full recovery takes six to eight weeks. Liver volume typically returns to near-normal within three months.
