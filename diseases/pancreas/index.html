Overview

Understanding Pancreatic Disease

The pancreas does two unrelated jobs. Its exocrine tissue makes the enzymes that digest fat and protein, delivered into the duodenum through a duct it shares with the bile system. Its endocrine islets make insulin and glucagon, released directly into the blood. Disease in one function frequently damages the other.

Acute pancreatitis is what happens when those digestive enzymes activate inside the gland rather than in the intestine. Gallstones and alcohol account for the large majority of cases. The presentation is unmistakable — severe upper abdominal pain boring through to the back, with vomiting — and the early priorities are aggressive fluid resuscitation, pain control and identifying the cause.

Chronic pancreatitis is a different disease with the same name. Repeated inflammation scars the gland permanently, producing relentless pain, fatty stools from enzyme failure, weight loss and eventually diabetes. Treatment addresses each of those separately: enzyme replacement, structured pain management, and drainage or resection surgery when the duct is obstructed.

Symptoms

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

None of these confirms a diagnosis on its own. Each is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see how things develop.

Severe Epigastric Pain

Constant, boring pain in the upper abdomen radiating straight through to the back, often eased slightly by leaning forward.

Persistent Vomiting

Repeated vomiting that brings no relief, accompanied by an inability to keep fluids down and rapid dehydration.

Steatorrhoea

Pale, bulky, greasy stools that are difficult to flush — the hallmark of exocrine failure, where fat passes through undigested.

Progressive Weight Loss

Losing weight despite eating, because enzymes are insufficient to extract nutrition from food that is being consumed normally.

New-Onset Diabetes

Diabetes appearing after fifty without family history, particularly with weight loss, which warrants imaging of the pancreas.

Fever and Rapid Pulse

Systemic signs during an acute attack, indicating an inflammatory response that requires inpatient monitoring.

Causes & Risk

Causes and Risk Factors

Understanding what drives the condition is what makes prevention and early detection practical rather than theoretical.

Causes

  1. Gallstone MigrationA stone passing through the shared channel obstructs the pancreatic duct and triggers acute pancreatitis — the commonest cause in India.
  2. AlcoholSustained heavy intake causes both acute attacks and, over years, the progressive fibrosis and duct changes of chronic pancreatitis.
  3. Metabolic CausesSeverely raised triglycerides and hypercalcaemia are less common but important, because both are correctable once identified.
  4. Structural and Autoimmune DiseasePancreas divisum, duct strictures and autoimmune pancreatitis, the last of which responds to steroids rather than surgery.

Risk Factors

  1. Known Gallstone DiseaseUntreated symptomatic gallstones are the single largest avoidable risk, and cholecystectomy after biliary pancreatitis prevents recurrence.
  2. Long-Term Alcohol UseRisk relates to cumulative intake over years, and continued drinking after a first attack markedly accelerates progression to chronic disease.
  3. SmokingAn independent risk factor for chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer, and one that compounds the effect of alcohol substantially.
  4. Raised TriglyceridesLevels above 1000 mg/dL can precipitate acute pancreatitis directly and require specific lipid-lowering management.
Diagnosis

How We Reach a Diagnosis

Only the investigations that will change a decision are ordered, and they are sequenced so results build on one another rather than repeating.

Serum Amylase and Lipase

Levels three times above normal establish acute pancreatitis. Lipase remains elevated longer and is the more reliable of the two.

Ultrasound

Performed early to identify gallstones as the cause, which changes management immediately even when the pancreas itself is obscured by bowel gas.

Contrast-Enhanced CT

Best performed after seventy-two hours, when it accurately shows necrosis, fluid collections and vascular complications.

MRCP

Maps the pancreatic and bile ducts without instrumentation, identifying stones, strictures and anatomical variants such as pancreas divisum.

Endoscopic Ultrasound

Characterises cystic lesions, samples fluid for analysis and detects small tumours that cross-sectional imaging cannot resolve.

Treatment

Treatment Options

Treatment is matched to the stage and to the individual. Not every option below applies to every patient, and the right choice is made together.

Emergency

Acute Resuscitation

Aggressive intravenous fluids, effective analgesia and early nutrition — the interventions that most influence outcome in severe acute pancreatitis.

Endoscopic

ERCP and Duct Clearance

Urgent stone extraction in biliary pancreatitis with cholangitis, and stenting for duct strictures in chronic disease.

Medical

Enzyme Replacement and Pain Control

Pancreatic enzyme supplements taken with meals to reverse malabsorption, alongside a structured multimodal approach to chronic pain.

Surgical

Drainage and Resection Procedures

Lateral pancreaticojejunostomy for an obstructed dilated duct, and resection where disease is confined to the head or tail of the gland.
