Overview

Understanding Colorectal Disease

The colon and rectum form the final two metres of the digestive tract, where water is reclaimed and waste is stored before it leaves. Most disease here is benign — polyps, diverticula, haemorrhoids, inflammation — and much of it can be diagnosed and treated during a single colonoscopy.

Colorectal cancer is unusual among cancers in that it has a visible, removable precursor. Almost all of it begins as an adenomatous polyp, which takes years to become malignant. That long window is precisely why screening works so well here: taking out the polyp prevents the cancer entirely rather than catching it early.

The symptoms that matter are unglamorous and easy to normalise. A change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks, blood in the stool, or iron-deficiency anaemia in an adult are each sufficient reason for a colonoscopy. Attributing them to piles or diet without looking is the commonest route to a late diagnosis.

Symptoms

Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

None of these confirms a diagnosis on its own. Each is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see how things develop.

Change in Bowel Habit

New constipation, looser stools or alternating between the two, persisting beyond a few weeks and representing a genuine departure from your normal.

Rectal Bleeding

Fresh blood or darker blood mixed into the stool. Common causes are benign, but bleeding is never something to assume away without examination.

Abdominal Pain and Cramping

Colicky pain, often with bloating, which can indicate diverticular inflammation or a partially obstructing lesion.

Unexplained Anaemia

Iron deficiency in an adult, particularly a man or a post-menopausal woman, implies slow blood loss from the gut until proven otherwise.

Incomplete Evacuation

A persistent sensation of needing to pass stool that is not relieved, characteristic of a mass in the rectum.

Unintentional Weight Loss

Weight loss with any of the above raises concern considerably and should prompt investigation without delay.

Causes & Risk

Causes and Risk Factors

Understanding what drives the condition is what makes prevention and early detection practical rather than theoretical.

Causes

  1. Adenomatous PolypsBenign growths in the bowel lining that accumulate genetic changes over years and are the origin of the great majority of colorectal cancers.
  2. Diverticular DiseaseSmall pouches herniating through the colon wall, associated with low dietary fibre, which can become inflamed, bleed or perforate.
  3. Inflammatory Bowel DiseaseUlcerative colitis and Crohn's disease causing chronic immune-mediated inflammation, with a cancer risk that rises with duration and extent.
  4. Functional DisordersIrritable bowel syndrome producing genuine and often severe symptoms without structural abnormality — a diagnosis made after exclusion, not instead of investigation.

Risk Factors

  1. Age Over FiftyThe dominant risk factor. Incidence rises sharply from the fifth decade, which is why guidelines now recommend screening from forty-five.
  2. Family HistoryA first-degree relative with colorectal cancer roughly doubles risk and brings forward the age at which screening should begin.
  3. Diet and LifestyleHigh intake of red and processed meat, low fibre, obesity, smoking and heavy alcohol each contribute independently to risk.
  4. Genetic SyndromesLynch syndrome and familial adenomatous polyposis carry very high lifetime risk and require intensive surveillance from early adulthood.
Diagnosis

How We Reach a Diagnosis

Only the investigations that will change a decision are ordered, and they are sequenced so results build on one another rather than repeating.

Blood Tests

Full blood count for iron-deficiency anaemia, inflammatory markers, and CEA where a cancer diagnosis is being followed.

Stool Testing

Faecal immunochemical testing detects occult blood and is an effective first-line screening tool in average-risk populations.

Colonoscopy

The definitive investigation. It visualises the entire colon, removes polyps in the same sitting and takes biopsies where needed.

CT Scan

Staging for confirmed cancer, and the assessment of choice for acute complications such as perforation or obstruction.

Pelvic MRI

Essential for rectal cancer. It defines the tumour's relationship to the mesorectal margin and determines whether treatment precedes surgery.

Treatment

Treatment Options

Treatment is matched to the stage and to the individual. Not every option below applies to every patient, and the right choice is made together.

Endoscopic

Polypectomy

Removal of polyps during colonoscopy, which interrupts the sequence to cancer before it can begin. No incision, no admission.

Medical

Inflammatory Disease Management

Targeted anti-inflammatory and biologic therapy for ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease, with surgery held in reserve.

Minimally Invasive

Laparoscopic Colectomy

Keyhole removal of the affected segment for diverticular disease, large polyps or cancer, with faster recovery and less pain than open surgery.

Complex

Total Mesorectal Excision

The precision operation for rectal cancer, removing the rectum within its intact fascial envelope, with sphincter preservation wherever the tumour allows.
