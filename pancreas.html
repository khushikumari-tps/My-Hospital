The Programme

A small gland with an outsized role in your health

The pancreas sits deep in the abdomen, behind the stomach. It does two jobs at once: it produces the enzymes that break down fat, protein and carbohydrate, and it releases the insulin and glucagon that hold your blood sugar steady. When it is inflamed or obstructed, both jobs fail quietly — which is why pancreatic disease is so often found late.

PancreaCare+ is Advitya Healthcares' dedicated pancreatic programme. It brings hepato-pancreato-biliary surgeons, gastroenterologists, radiologists, oncologists and dietitians around a single table, so that diagnosis is precise, treatment is planned once, and follow-up does not depend on which department you happen to reach.

Conditions We Treat

The full spectrum of pancreatic disease

From a single acute attack to complex resections — each condition is managed by the same specialist team, using one shared treatment plan.

Acute Pancreatitis

Sudden inflammation, most often caused by gallstones or alcohol. Severe cases need immediate fluid resuscitation, pain control and monitoring for organ failure or necrosis.

Emergency care

Chronic Pancreatitis

Persistent inflammation that scars the gland and slowly reduces enzyme and hormone production. Managed with pain control, enzyme replacement, diet and duct drainage where needed.

Long-term care

Pancreatic Cancer

Most commonly adenocarcinoma of the duct lining; less often a neuroendocrine tumour. Staged with CT, EUS and biopsy, then treated by surgery, chemotherapy or a combination.

Surgical oncology

Fatty Pancreas

Fat infiltrating the gland, usually alongside obesity, diabetes or fatty liver. Often silent, but linked to inflammation and reduced function — managed with metabolic and lifestyle care.

Metabolic

Pancreatic Cysts

Serous cystadenomas, IPMNs, mucinous cystic neoplasms and pseudocysts. Some need only surveillance; IPMNs and MCNs carry a risk of turning cancerous and may need removal.

Surveillance

Autoimmune Pancreatitis

An immune-driven inflammation that can closely mimic pancreatic cancer on imaging. Accurate distinction matters — it usually responds well to steroid therapy rather than surgery.

Immunology

Hereditary Pancreatitis

Inherited gene changes such as PRSS1 that cause repeated inflammation from a young age. Requires genetic counselling, family screening and lifelong cancer surveillance.

Genetic
Warning Signs

Six symptoms worth a specialist opinion

Individually these are common and often harmless. Together, or persisting beyond a couple of weeks, they deserve a proper pancreatic assessment.

Abdominal pain

Persistent pain in the upper abdomen, often worse after eating or lying flat.

Back pain

Pain that radiates from the upper abdomen straight through to the mid-back.

Jaundice

Yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine and pale stools — a sign of bile duct obstruction.

Unexplained weight loss

Losing weight without trying, often alongside oily or floating stools.

Loss of appetite

Feeling full quickly, or a persistent lack of interest in food with nausea.

New-onset diabetes

Diabetes appearing for the first time after 50, particularly with weight loss.

Frequently Asked

Questions patients ask us most

What are the earliest warning signs of a pancreatic problem?

Persistent upper abdominal pain radiating to the back, unexplained weight loss, jaundice, greasy or floating stools, and new-onset diabetes after 50. Any of these lasting more than a couple of weeks deserves a specialist review — early pancreatic disease is often silent, which is why symptoms that persist matter more than symptoms that are severe.

How is pancreatic disease diagnosed?

Diagnosis is layered rather than a single test:

  • Blood tests for pancreatic enzymes, liver function and tumour markers such as CA 19-9
  • Ultrasound first, then high-resolution CT, MRI or MRCP to map the gland and ducts
  • Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) for close-range imaging and tissue sampling
  • ERCP where a duct needs to be cleared or stented as well as examined
Is pancreatic surgery safe, and who should perform it?

Pancreatic surgery is major surgery, and outcomes depend strongly on how often the team performs it. High-volume centres with fellowship-trained hepato-pancreato-biliary surgeons report substantially lower complication rates than occasional operators. Look for a specialist surgeon, a centre that does this work routinely, and a multidisciplinary team including gastroenterologists, dietitians and endocrinologists.

How long is recovery after major pancreatic surgery?

Most patients stay in hospital for 7–14 days depending on the procedure and their condition on admission. Returning to routine activity typically takes 6–8 weeks, with diet and enzyme support guided by our nutrition team throughout. Fatigue and digestive adjustment are normal in the first months.

Will I become diabetic after pancreatic surgery?

It depends on how much of the gland is removed. A limited resection often leaves enough insulin-producing tissue behind. A total pancreatectomy always causes diabetes, so insulin therapy and enzyme replacement are planned from the outset with an endocrinologist and dietitian.

Can chronic pancreatitis be cured?

No — the scarring it causes is permanent. With proper management, however, symptoms can be controlled and complications minimised: stopping alcohol and smoking, structured pain control, enzyme supplements, a low-fat and nutrient-dense diet, and procedures to drain blocked ducts or pseudocysts where needed.

Do you review reports before I travel for an appointment?

Yes. For outstation patients, a video review of your existing scans and reports is usually the first step, so the trip is made only when it will change something. Bring the actual films or CD rather than only the printed report, along with previous prescriptions, discharge summaries and blood work from the last three months.

Do you accept insurance, and can I get a cost estimate first?

We work with most major insurers and TPAs. Share your policy details at least 48 hours before a planned admission so pre-authorisation can be cleared in time; for emergencies the desk starts the claim while treatment begins. Once the treating consultant confirms the plan, the billing desk issues a written estimate covering surgery, stay, consumables and expected investigations.

Understanding the Normal Pancreas: A Q&A Guide

11 questions · anatomy, function and staying healthy
What is the pancreas, and where is it located?

The pancreas is a vital gland located deep in the abdomen. It sits behind the stomach and is nestled between the liver, spleen and small intestine. Shaped like a flat, elongated fish, it has three main parts:

  • Head: tucked into the curve of the small intestine
  • Body: the central portion behind the stomach
  • Tail: the tapered end near the spleen
What does the pancreas do?

The pancreas plays two critical roles:

  • Digestion (exocrine function): it produces digestive enzymes to break down fats, proteins and carbohydrates, ensuring your body absorbs nutrients efficiently
  • Blood sugar regulation (endocrine function): it releases hormones like insulin and glucagon to keep blood sugar levels in check
What are pancreatic digestive enzymes, and why are they important?

The pancreas produces three main types of enzyme:

  • Amylase: breaks down carbohydrates into sugars
  • Lipase: helps digest dietary fats
  • Proteases (like trypsin and chymotrypsin): break down proteins into amino acids

These enzymes are crucial for nutrient absorption and energy production. Without them, food would pass through your system without providing nourishment.

How does the pancreas regulate blood sugar?

The pancreas secretes hormones into the bloodstream:

  • Insulin: helps cells absorb glucose, reducing blood sugar levels
  • Glucagon: signals the liver to release stored glucose, raising blood sugar levels
  • Somatostatin: acts as a regulator, ensuring a balance between insulin and glucagon

This delicate balance keeps your energy levels stable throughout the day.

How does the pancreas interact with other organs?
  • With the stomach: releases digestive enzymes when food enters the small intestine
  • With the liver: regulates blood sugar levels through glucagon and insulin
  • With the intestines: helps neutralise stomach acid and facilitates digestion

Together, these systems ensure smooth digestion and metabolism.

What does a healthy pancreas look like in action?

A healthy pancreas operates silently and seamlessly. You likely have a well-functioning pancreas if:

  • You digest food without bloating or discomfort
  • Your blood sugar levels remain stable
  • You maintain energy and a healthy weight
Can the pancreas heal itself if damaged?

The pancreas has some ability to recover, particularly from mild inflammation such as acute pancreatitis. However, chronic damage — due to repeated inflammation, alcohol misuse or other factors — can lead to irreversible scarring and loss of function. Preventive care is key to protecting this organ.

How can you keep your pancreas healthy?

Practical tips for pancreatic health:

  • Eat a balanced diet: focus on fresh fruits, vegetables, whole grains and lean proteins
  • Limit alcohol and avoid smoking: both are major risk factors for pancreatic damage
  • Stay active: regular exercise supports overall health and weight management
  • Stay hydrated: proper hydration supports digestion and enzyme activity
  • Monitor your health: regular checkups can identify early signs of issues
What are some common tests to check pancreatic health?
  • Blood tests: to measure enzyme and blood sugar levels
  • Imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI): to visualise the pancreas and surrounding structures
  • Endoscopic tests (e.g. ERCP): to examine the pancreatic ducts and detect blockages

Early diagnosis can prevent complications and improve treatment outcomes.

Why is it important to understand the pancreas?

Understanding your pancreas can empower you to recognise early warning signs of issues like diabetes, pancreatitis or pancreatic cancer. Awareness is the first step toward prevention and timely care.

In conclusion

The pancreas may be small, but its impact on health is immense. Protecting it through a healthy lifestyle and regular checkups can go a long way toward preventing disease and ensuring long-term wellness.

Understanding Acute Severe Pancreatitis: A Patient's Guide

8 questions · causes, symptoms, treatment and outlook
What is acute severe pancreatitis?

Acute severe pancreatitis (ASP) is an inflammation of the pancreas that occurs suddenly and can cause severe complications. The pancreas is a vital organ responsible for producing digestive enzymes and regulating blood sugar. In ASP, the inflammation can damage not only the pancreas but also surrounding tissues and organs.

What causes acute severe pancreatitis?
  • Gallstones: these block the bile duct, leading to a build-up of pancreatic enzymes, which then damage the pancreas
  • Alcohol consumption: heavy alcohol use can trigger inflammation in the pancreas
  • Other causes: certain medications, infections, high triglyceride levels or trauma
What are the symptoms of acute severe pancreatitis?

Symptoms can be intense and sudden. They include:

  • Severe abdominal pain, usually in the upper abdomen and radiating to the back
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Fever
  • Rapid heartbeat
  • Swollen or tender abdomen

Seek medical attention immediately — acute severe pancreatitis can escalate quickly.

How is acute severe pancreatitis diagnosed?
  • Blood tests: assess levels of pancreatic enzymes (amylase and lipase), which are typically elevated in pancreatitis
  • Imaging tests: a CT scan, MRI or ultrasound to confirm the diagnosis and assess the severity of the inflammation
How is acute severe pancreatitis treated medically?

ASP requires hospitalisation and immediate medical care. Medical management includes:

  • Fluid resuscitation: intravenous (IV) fluids are essential to maintain blood pressure and hydration
  • Pain management: strong pain relief is often needed to manage the intense abdominal pain
  • Nutrition: eating is often withheld initially to rest the pancreas. In severe cases, nutrition may be given through a feeding tube
  • Antibiotics: ASP is not always caused by infection, but antibiotics may be used if an infection is suspected
What is the role of endoscopic treatment?

Endoscopic treatments can be essential, particularly when ASP is caused by gallstones or a blockage in the bile duct:

  • ERCP: used to remove gallstones or relieve bile duct blockages. In severe cases, ERCP can prevent complications
  • Drainage of pancreatic fluid collections: endoscopic procedures may be used to drain fluid or abscesses that have formed around the pancreas
What complications can arise from acute severe pancreatitis?
  • Organ failure: the condition can affect the lungs, kidneys or heart, leading to multi-organ failure
  • Pancreatic necrosis: parts of the pancreas die, leading to infection or abscesses
  • Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS): a widespread inflammatory reaction that can cause shock and other systemic issues
What is the outlook for patients with acute severe pancreatitis?

The outlook depends on how quickly treatment is initiated and the severity of the condition. With timely medical care many patients recover, but severe cases can involve a prolonged hospital stay and may require intensive care. Long-term complications may include diabetes or chronic pancreatitis if extensive pancreatic damage occurs.

Understanding Biliary Pancreatitis: A Patient's Guide

13 questions · gallstones, ERCP and surgery
What causes biliary pancreatitis?

The most common cause is gallstones, which form in the gallbladder and can move into the bile duct. When these stones get stuck, they obstruct the flow of bile and pancreatic fluids, leading to inflammation of the pancreas. Sometimes narrowing or swelling of the bile duct can also contribute.

What are the symptoms of biliary pancreatitis?
  • Severe pain in the upper abdomen, which may radiate to the back
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Fever and chills
  • Jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes)
  • Bloating or a distended abdomen

Seek medical attention immediately — untreated pancreatitis can lead to serious complications.

How is biliary pancreatitis diagnosed?

Diagnosis typically involves blood tests to check for elevated pancreatic enzymes and liver function tests. Imaging studies such as abdominal ultrasound, CT scan or MRI may be used to visualise the gallstones and the inflammation in the pancreas. Sometimes an endoscopic procedure called ERCP is used both to diagnose and to treat the condition.

How is biliary pancreatitis treated?

The first step is usually hospitalisation to manage symptoms such as pain, nausea and dehydration. The patient is given IV fluids and may need to stop eating temporarily to let the pancreas rest. If gallstones are the cause, they may be removed through an ERCP procedure. In some cases surgery is needed to remove the gallbladder and prevent future episodes.

What is the initial medical treatment?

Treatment begins with hospitalisation to stabilise the patient:

  • Fluid resuscitation: IV fluids maintain hydration and blood pressure, which can drop due to the inflammation
  • Pain management: acetaminophen, NSAIDs or opioids may be used to control pain
  • Fasting (NPO status): patients are usually advised to stop eating and drinking to allow the pancreas to rest
  • Antibiotics: not routinely prescribed for acute pancreatitis, but given if there is evidence of infection
Can biliary pancreatitis resolve without further treatment?

In mild cases, symptoms may improve with conservative medical management alone. However, without addressing the underlying cause — typically a gallstone — the risk of recurrence is high.

What is ERCP, and what role does endoscopy play?

ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography) is a minimally invasive procedure often performed when a gallstone is stuck in the bile duct.

It combines endoscopy and X-ray to visualise the bile and pancreatic ducts. During the procedure a doctor can remove the obstructing stone or place a stent to ensure proper bile drainage.

When is ERCP performed, and is it safe?

When: ERCP is usually indicated if there is persistent blockage, cholangitis (bile duct infection) or jaundice. It is often performed within 24–48 hours of diagnosis when these complications are present.

Safety: while generally safe, ERCP carries some risk of infection, bleeding or even inducing pancreatitis. In expert hands, the benefits of relieving bile duct obstruction usually outweigh the risks.

When is surgery necessary?

Surgery may be required for patients with severe or recurrent biliary pancreatitis, particularly if gallstones are the cause.

  • Cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal): once the acute phase has passed, this is often recommended to prevent future episodes. Removing the gallbladder eliminates the source of gallstones and significantly reduces recurrence risk
  • Timing of surgery: in mild cases, cholecystectomy can be performed during the same hospital stay once inflammation has subsided. In more severe cases surgery may be delayed until the patient is stable
Are there other surgical options?

If the gallbladder is not the cause, or if complications like necrosis or infection occur, more complex surgery may be needed — such as drainage of pancreatic fluid collections or removal of damaged tissue.

What is the long-term outlook?

The prognosis depends on severity. In mild cases patients recover within a few days with appropriate treatment. In more severe cases, complications such as infection, pancreatic necrosis (tissue death) or organ failure may occur. Removing the gallbladder usually prevents future episodes of biliary pancreatitis.

Can biliary pancreatitis be prevented?

The best way is to reduce the risk of gallstones, the leading cause:

  • Maintain a healthy weight
  • Eat a balanced diet that is low in fat and high in fibre
  • Stay physically active

If you are prone to gallstones or have had previous episodes, your doctor may recommend gallbladder removal to prevent future attacks.

When should I see a doctor?

Seek medical attention immediately if you experience severe abdominal pain, especially with nausea, vomiting, fever or jaundice. These could be signs of biliary pancreatitis or another serious condition that requires urgent care.

Understanding Chronic Pancreatitis: A Patient's Guide

12 questions · causes, complications, diet and living with it
What is chronic pancreatitis?

Chronic pancreatitis is a persistent inflammation of the pancreas that leads to irreversible damage over time. The inflamed pancreas becomes scarred and loses its ability to produce enzymes and hormones, affecting digestion and blood sugar regulation.

How is it different from acute pancreatitis?

Acute pancreatitis is a sudden inflammation that often resolves with treatment. Chronic pancreatitis develops gradually and leads to permanent damage, causing recurring pain, digestive issues and sometimes diabetes.

What causes chronic pancreatitis?
  • Alcohol abuse: a leading cause in adults
  • Genetic factors: inherited conditions like cystic fibrosis or mutations in genes such as PRSS1
  • Gallstones: can lead to repeated inflammation
  • Autoimmune conditions: the immune system mistakenly attacks the pancreas
  • Idiopathic causes: in some cases no clear cause is identified
What are the symptoms of chronic pancreatitis?
  • Persistent or recurring abdominal pain: typically in the upper abdomen, radiating to the back
  • Weight loss: due to poor digestion and nutrient absorption
  • Steatorrhea (oily stools): a sign of fat malabsorption
  • Nausea and vomiting
How is chronic pancreatitis diagnosed?
  • Medical history and symptoms: assessing risk factors like alcohol use or family history
  • Imaging tests: CT scans, MRI or endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) to detect pancreatic damage or calcifications
  • Blood tests: to check enzyme levels and rule out other conditions
  • Stool tests: to evaluate fat malabsorption
What are the complications of chronic pancreatitis?
  • Pancreatic insufficiency: reduced enzyme production causing malabsorption
  • Diabetes: from loss of insulin-producing cells
  • Pancreatic pseudocysts: fluid-filled sacs that can cause pain or infection
  • Pancreatic cancer: increased risk, especially in long-standing cases
What are the treatment options?

Treatment focuses on managing symptoms and preventing complications:

  • Lifestyle changes: stop alcohol consumption immediately; quit smoking
  • Pain management: using medications or, in severe cases, nerve blocks
  • Digestive enzymes: supplements to improve digestion and nutrient absorption
  • Insulin therapy: if diabetes develops
  • Dietary adjustments: a low-fat, nutrient-rich diet tailored by a dietitian
  • Surgery or endoscopic procedures: to address complications like blocked ducts or pseudocysts
How can diet help manage chronic pancreatitis?

Diet plays a crucial role. Eating the right foods reduces strain on the pancreas and improves digestion:

  • Low-fat foods: minimise enzyme workload and prevent triggering pain or discomfort
  • Small, frequent meals: easier to digest and less likely to overwhelm the pancreas
  • Nutrient-dense options: lean proteins (chicken, fish, tofu), whole grains, and plenty of fruit and vegetables
  • Hydration: drink plenty of water to support digestion

Avoid fried foods, high-fat meals and alcohol. Consulting a dietitian helps tailor a plan to your needs.

Can chronic pancreatitis be cured?

There is no cure for chronic pancreatitis. However, with proper management, symptoms can be controlled and complications minimised. Adhering to medical advice and making lifestyle changes are crucial for improving quality of life.

What can you do to prevent chronic pancreatitis?
  • Avoid alcohol and smoking: key risk factors for inflammation and damage
  • Maintain a healthy diet: low in fat and processed foods
  • Manage underlying conditions: such as gallstones or genetic disorders
  • Stay hydrated and active: supports overall pancreatic health
When should you see a doctor?
  • Severe or recurring abdominal pain
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Fatty or foul-smelling stools
  • Persistent nausea or vomiting

Early intervention can help manage the condition and prevent complications.

How does chronic pancreatitis affect mental health?

Living with chronic pain and dietary restrictions can lead to stress, anxiety or depression. Seeking support from healthcare professionals, counsellors or support groups can make a big difference.

Understanding Pancreatic Cancer: A Patient's Guide

9 questions · types, risk, staging and treatment
What is pancreatic cancer?

Pancreatic cancer occurs when abnormal cells grow uncontrollably in the pancreas, a vital organ located behind the stomach. The pancreas has two primary functions: producing enzymes that aid digestion and hormones that regulate blood sugar. Most pancreatic cancers begin in the cells lining the ducts of the pancreas (adenocarcinoma), but others can develop in hormone-producing or enzyme-producing cells.

What are the types of pancreatic cancer?
  • Exocrine tumours (adenocarcinoma): the most common type, affecting the part of the pancreas involved in digestion
  • Endocrine tumours (neuroendocrine tumours): less common, arising from hormone-producing cells in the pancreas
What are the risk factors for pancreatic cancer?
  • Age: most cases occur in people over 60
  • Family history: a family history of pancreatic cancer or certain genetic syndromes increases the risk
  • Smoking: one of the most significant risk factors
  • Chronic pancreatitis: long-term inflammation can lead to cancer
  • Diabetes: particularly in people diagnosed with diabetes later in life
What are the early symptoms of pancreatic cancer?

Early-stage pancreatic cancer may not cause noticeable symptoms, making it difficult to detect. As it progresses:

  • Abdominal pain: often radiating to the back
  • Weight loss: unintended and significant
  • Jaundice: yellowing of the skin and eyes, caused by bile duct obstruction
  • Digestive problems: nausea, vomiting and indigestion due to poor enzyme production
  • Loss of appetite: a reduced desire to eat

If these symptoms persist or worsen, consult your doctor for further evaluation.

How is pancreatic cancer diagnosed?
  • Imaging tests: CT scans, MRIs and ultrasounds give detailed images of the pancreas
  • Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS): a scope with an ultrasound probe is inserted into the stomach to capture images of the pancreas
  • Biopsy: a small tissue sample is taken to confirm the diagnosis
  • Blood tests: markers such as CA 19-9 may be elevated, though this is not always reliable for early diagnosis
What are the stages of pancreatic cancer?
  • Stage 1: the cancer is confined to the pancreas
  • Stage 2: spread to nearby tissues but not to major blood vessels or distant organs
  • Stage 3: involves nearby blood vessels but has not spread to distant parts of the body
  • Stage 4: metastasised to other organs such as the liver or lungs
What are the treatment options for pancreatic cancer?

Surgery — if detected early, surgery can remove the cancerous part of the pancreas:

  • Whipple procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy): removes the head of the pancreas, part of the stomach, the bile duct and part of the small intestine
  • Distal pancreatectomy: removes the body and tail of the pancreas

Alongside surgery:

  • Chemotherapy: often used before or after surgery to kill cancer cells or shrink tumours
  • Radiation therapy: may be used alongside chemotherapy to target cancer cells
  • Targeted therapy: for specific mutations, drugs attack cancer cells without harming normal cells
  • Immunotherapy: in some cases, boosts the immune system's ability to fight cancer
What is the role of palliative care in pancreatic cancer?

Because pancreatic cancer is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, palliative care plays a crucial role in improving quality of life. Palliative treatments focus on managing symptoms such as pain, digestive issues and jaundice, as well as emotional support for patients and their families.

Can pancreatic cancer be prevented?

It is not always possible to prevent pancreatic cancer, but certain lifestyle changes may lower your risk:

  • Quit smoking: smoking is a major risk factor
  • Maintain a healthy diet: rich in fruits, vegetables and whole grains
  • Limit alcohol: excessive consumption can lead to chronic pancreatitis, itself a risk factor
  • Exercise regularly: maintaining a healthy weight reduces risk

Surgery for Pancreatic Cancer

5 questions · eligibility, procedures and recovery
Who qualifies for pancreatic cancer surgery?

Not all patients with pancreatic cancer are candidates for surgery. Whether surgery is an option depends on the size and location of the tumour, whether it has spread, and the overall health of the patient. Typically, patients with localised or borderline resectable cancer are considered for surgery. Those with advanced cancer that has spread to distant organs usually require other treatments, such as chemotherapy or radiation.

What are the common types of surgery for pancreatic cancer?
  • Whipple procedure (pancreatoduodenectomy): the most common surgery, performed when the cancer is in the head of the pancreas. It removes part of the pancreas, the duodenum, the gallbladder, part of the stomach and sometimes nearby lymph nodes
  • Distal pancreatectomy: for tumours in the tail, removing the tail and sometimes a portion of the body of the pancreas, often along with the spleen
  • Total pancreatectomy: in rare cases the entire pancreas is removed. This may be necessary for extensive tumours but results in diabetes, since the pancreas produces insulin
How effective is surgery for pancreatic cancer?

Surgery offers the best chance for long-term survival, especially when combined with other treatments like chemotherapy and radiation. Effectiveness depends on the stage of cancer, the patient's response to treatment, and whether the tumour can be completely removed. Even after surgery, pancreatic cancer has a high rate of recurrence, which is why post-surgical treatments are important.

What is recovery like after surgery?

Recovery from pancreatic surgery can be lengthy. Hospital stays typically range from one to two weeks, and full recovery may take several months. Patients may experience fatigue, digestive issues and pain as they heal. Follow-up care, including nutritional support and monitoring for recurrence, is essential.

Can surgery be combined with other treatments?

Yes. Many patients receive chemotherapy or radiation either before surgery (neoadjuvant therapy) to shrink the tumour, or after surgery (adjuvant therapy) to reduce the risk of recurrence. The decision is based on the cancer's stage and the patient's overall health.

Non-Cancerous Pancreatic Tumours

8 questions · cysts, IPMNs, NETs and surveillance
What are non-cancerous pancreatic tumours?

Non-cancerous pancreatic tumours are growths in the pancreas that do not spread to other parts of the body. Unlike cancerous tumours they are usually less aggressive, but may still require medical attention depending on their size, location and impact on pancreatic function.

What are the common types?
  • Serous cystadenomas (SCAs): fluid-filled cysts that are typically non-symptomatic and rarely cause complications
  • Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs): cystic tumours in the pancreatic ducts that may become cancerous if left untreated
  • Mucinous cystic neoplasms (MCNs): more common in women, and can sometimes progress to cancer
  • Solid pseudopapillary tumours (SPTs): rare tumours often found in younger women, generally non-cancerous but may require surgical removal
  • Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs): often benign, but can produce hormones leading to symptoms such as low blood sugar or ulcers
What causes them?

The exact cause is often unknown, but risk factors may include:

  • Genetic mutations: inherited conditions like von Hippel-Lindau disease or MEN1 syndrome
  • Chronic pancreatitis: long-term inflammation may increase the likelihood of benign growth
  • Hormonal imbalances: overproduction of certain hormones may trigger tumour growth
What are the symptoms?
  • Abdominal pain or discomfort
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Jaundice, if the tumour obstructs bile flow
  • Digestive issues like bloating or fatty stools
  • Low blood sugar, ulcers or diarrhoea, for hormone-producing tumours

Some tumours are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during imaging for other conditions.

Do they require treatment?
  • Observation: small, asymptomatic tumours may only require regular monitoring
  • Surgery: recommended for larger tumours, symptomatic cases, or those with a risk of becoming cancerous
  • Hormonal therapy: for hormone-producing tumours, medication may help manage symptoms

Your doctor will decide the best course of action based on the individual case.

What are the risks of leaving one untreated?
  • Growth that leads to pressure on surrounding organs or ducts
  • Development of symptoms like pain or digestive problems
  • Progression to cancer in certain types, such as IPMNs or MCNs

Regular follow-up with your doctor is crucial to monitor any changes.

Are they linked to pancreatic cancer?

Some benign tumours, like IPMNs and MCNs, carry a risk of turning cancerous over time. Early detection and regular monitoring are crucial to preventing progression. However, most benign tumours do not develop into cancer.

When should I see a doctor about a pancreatic tumour?
  • Persistent abdominal pain
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Digestive issues or changes in stool
  • Jaundice or other unexplained symptoms

If you are diagnosed with a pancreatic tumour, follow your doctor's recommendations for treatment or monitoring.

08

Surgeries for the Pancreas

8 questions · procedures, risks and choosing a centre
Why might someone need pancreatic surgery?
  • Pancreatic cancer: to remove cancerous tissue
  • Chronic pancreatitis: to relieve pain, manage complications or remove damaged portions of the pancreas
  • Benign tumours or cysts: such as MCNs or IPMNs, which may risk becoming cancerous
  • Injuries: from trauma to the pancreas
  • Blockages: in the bile duct or pancreatic duct
What are the most common types of pancreatic surgery?
  • Whipple procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy): removes the head of the pancreas, part of the small intestine, the gallbladder and the bile duct. Often used for cancer in the pancreatic head or surrounding structures
  • Distal pancreatectomy: removes the tail of the pancreas and sometimes the spleen. Used for tumours or cysts in the pancreatic tail
  • Total pancreatectomy: removes the entire pancreas, gallbladder, parts of the stomach and small intestine. Used for widespread disease or some hereditary conditions
  • Central pancreatectomy: removes the middle portion while preserving the head and tail. Used for benign or low-grade tumours in the pancreatic body
  • Drainage procedures (e.g. Puestow procedure): create a connection between the pancreas and intestines to drain blocked ducts. Commonly used in chronic pancreatitis
How is laparoscopic or robotic surgery different from open surgery?
  • Laparoscopic / robotic surgery: minimally invasive techniques using small incisions and specialised tools. Benefits include shorter recovery time, less pain and smaller scars
  • Open surgery: larger incisions, typically used for more complex or extensive conditions

The choice depends on the specific case and the surgeon's expertise.

What are the risks associated with pancreatic surgery?
  • Bleeding or infection: common surgical risks
  • Digestive issues: due to reduced enzyme production
  • Diabetes: if a significant portion of the pancreas is removed
  • Delayed gastric emptying: slower digestion can occur temporarily
  • Leakage at surgical sites: from connections between the pancreas and other organs

Careful monitoring and expert surgical teams reduce these risks.

How does pancreatic surgery affect digestion?

The pancreas produces enzymes essential for digesting fats, proteins and carbohydrates. After surgery, reduced enzyme production can lead to:

  • Malabsorption or oily stools (steatorrhea)
  • Nutrient deficiencies

To manage this, doctors may prescribe pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) to aid digestion.

What lifestyle changes are needed after pancreas surgery?
  • Diet: focus on small, frequent, low-fat meals. Work with a dietitian for personalised guidance
  • Medications: regular use of enzyme supplements and, if needed, insulin
  • Regular exercise: helps manage blood sugar and overall health
  • Avoid alcohol and smoking: reduces strain on the pancreas and prevents complications
How do I choose the right hospital and surgeon?
  • Experience matters: seek surgeons who specialise in pancreatic procedures
  • High-volume centres: hospitals that perform many pancreatic surgeries often have better outcomes
  • Multidisciplinary care: look for a team approach that includes surgeons, gastroenterologists, dietitians and endocrinologists
What are the chances of success with pancreatic surgery?

Success depends on the underlying condition, the patient's health and the surgical team's expertise. Advances in techniques and post-operative care have significantly improved outcomes, particularly at specialised centres.
