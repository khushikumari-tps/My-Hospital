Pancreatic cancer is quiet until it is not.

A leaf-shaped gland behind the stomach, doing two jobs at once. Why the disease is so often found late, what the warning signs are, and what treatment actually involves.

Red flags — never ignore these

  • Unexplained weight loss or loss of appetite
  • Difficulty or pain with swallowing
  • Persistent indigestion, heartburn or abdominal pain
  • Vomiting blood, or coffee-ground-coloured material
  • Black, tarry or bloody stools
  • New jaundice — yellowing of the skin or eyes
  • Dark urine and pale stools
  • A change in bowel habit lasting more than three weeks
  • Unexplained anaemia
  • New diabetes after 50, with weight loss
  • An abdominal lump or swelling

What is it?

The pancreas is a leaf-shaped gland tucked behind the stomach, doing two unrelated jobs: producing the enzymes that digest food, and making the insulin that regulates blood sugar.

The commonest form is pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Neuroendocrine tumours and cystic tumours also occur, and they behave quite differently — which is why the distinction matters before any treatment is planned.

Why does it happen?

  • Tobacco — smoked or chewed
  • Chronic pancreatitis
  • New-onset diabetes after 50, particularly with weight loss
  • Obesity and a sedentary lifestyle
  • Heavy alcohol use
  • Family history or genetic mutations — BRCA2, PALB2, ATM, Lynch syndrome
  • Precancerous cysts, particularly high-risk IPMNs

How can I lower my risk?

  • Stop smoking. It is the single most effective step available.
  • Keep a healthy weight, with regular exercise.
  • Control blood sugar and cholesterol.
  • Limit alcohol.
  • If you carry a strong family history or a known mutation, structured surveillance with EUS or MRI every one to two years.

How is it diagnosed?

Pancreatic cancer is often caught late because the early symptoms are vague.

  • CT pancreas protocol — the main staging scan
  • MRI / MRCP — for duct detail
  • EUS with biopsy — the most accurate way to confirm the diagnosis
  • CA 19-9 — a blood marker, read alongside imaging rather than alone
  • PET scan — in selected cases

How is it treated?

Only around 15–20% of pancreatic cancers are found early enough for potentially curative surgery.

  • Resectable: surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy
  • Borderline resectable: chemotherapy first, then reassessment
  • Locally advanced or metastatic: chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy
  • Biliary stenting via ERCP to relieve jaundice
  • Coeliac plexus block for pain
  • Enzyme replacement and nutritional support throughout

The Whipple procedure

For cancers of the pancreatic head, the Whipple is one of the most complex abdominal operations performed. It removes the pancreatic head, the duodenum, the bile duct, the gallbladder and sometimes part of the stomach — then reconstructs three separate connections.

For tumours of the body or tail, a distal pancreatectomy is used instead; for widespread disease, a total pancreatectomy. Hospital stay is typically 7–14 days, with recovery over 6–8 weeks.

Aftercare and recovery

  • Pancreatic enzyme replacement with every meal
  • Blood sugar monitoring — diabetes can develop after surgery
  • Insulin therapy where needed
  • Dietitian input and nutritional support
  • Regular CT scans and CA 19-9 monitoring
  • Psychological support

Enzyme supplements are not optional after pancreatic surgery. Without them, food passes through undigested — causing weight loss, fatty stools and fatigue.
