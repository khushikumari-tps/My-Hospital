The tumour matters. So does the liver around it.

Primary liver cancer usually grows in a liver already damaged by scarring. That is why treatment depends as much on remaining liver function as on the size of the tumour.

What is it?

The liver is the largest internal organ, and it carries out a long list of essential jobs. Primary liver cancer — hepatocellular carcinoma, or HCC — develops within the liver cells themselves, which distinguishes it from secondary cancer that has spread there from elsewhere.

HCC typically emerges against a background of long-standing liver damage and scarring — cirrhosis.

Why does it happen?

  • Hepatitis B and hepatitis C infection
  • Alcohol-related liver disease leading to cirrhosis
  • Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, linked to obesity and diabetes
  • Cirrhosis from any cause
  • Aflatoxin exposure
  • Metabolic liver disease such as haemochromatosis

How can I lower my risk?

  • Hepatitis B vaccination
  • Testing for viral hepatitis, and treating it if found
  • Maintaining a healthy weight
  • Limiting alcohol
  • Avoiding substances toxic to the liver
  • If you have cirrhosis: six-monthly ultrasound surveillance

How is it diagnosed?

  • Liver ultrasound
  • Triphasic CT or MRI — timed contrast imaging that characterises the lesion
  • AFP blood testing
  • Liver biopsy, where imaging is not conclusive
  • Assessment of liver function, which shapes every subsequent decision

How is it treated?

  • Surgical resection
  • Liver transplantation
  • Ablation — radiofrequency or microwave
  • TACE and TARE — delivering treatment through the tumour’s blood supply
  • Systemic therapy — targeted agents or immunotherapy in advanced disease

Hepatectomy removes the tumour with a margin of healthy tissue. A healthy liver can regenerate up to about 70% of its volume, which is what makes major resection possible. The operation may be open or laparoscopic, with a typical stay of 4–7 days.

Aftercare and recovery

  • Lifelong abstinence from alcohol
  • Treating the underlying cause — viral hepatitis, metabolic disease
  • Surveillance imaging every three to six months
  • Managing the complications of cirrhosis
  • Nutritional support
  • Immunosuppression, for transplant recipients
