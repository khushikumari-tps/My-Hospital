Biliary Disease

Surgical repair of bile duct injuries

Bile duct injury is a rare complication of gallbladder surgery, occurring in roughly 0.3% to 0.6% of laparoscopic procedures. It is also one of the few situations where who performs the repair, and when, changes the outcome more than anything else.

Why is surgery essential for bile duct injuries?

  • Restores bile flow: ensures bile drains from the liver into the intestine.
  • Prevents complications: avoids long-term infection, bile leaks, strictures and liver damage.
  • Relieves symptoms: settles the jaundice, pain and digestive problems the injury causes.

Early surgical repair, performed by experienced hepatobiliary surgeons, has success rates of 85% to 95% in restoring normal bile flow and preventing complications.

What happens if the repair is done poorly?

  • Biliary strictures: scar tissue narrows the duct, causing recurrent jaundice and pain — seen in 25% to 40% of suboptimal repairs.
  • Persistent bile leaks: unaddressed leaks cause infection, peritonitis or abscess, and repeated interventions.
  • Cholangitis: recurrent bile duct infection, with mortality up to 10% in untreated cases.
  • Liver damage or failure: chronic obstruction can lead to cirrhosis, and some patients require transplantation.
  • Diminished quality of life: chronic pain, fatigue and frequent hospitalisation.

What drives poor outcomes?

  • Delayed diagnosis: waiting too long to identify and address the injury.
  • Improper technique: misaligned repairs, or failure to recognise the full extent of damage.
  • Inadequate expertise: general surgeons without hepatobiliary training face higher complication rates.
  • Lack of follow-up: missed chances to detect and manage complications early.

Centres with low experience in bile duct repair report failure rates of up to 50%, compared with 5% to 10% in high-volume specialised facilities.

How does expert surgical care improve outcomes?

  • Precise assessment: advanced imaging such as MRCP or ERCP for accurate evaluation of the injury.
  • Tailored technique: primary repair or stenting may suffice for minor injuries; Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy has a high success rate for complex ones.
  • Multidisciplinary approach: collaboration with gastroenterologists, interventional radiologists and infectious disease specialists.
  • Close post-operative monitoring: regular imaging and liver function tests to detect issues early.

Why does timing matter?

  • Early repair, within 72 hours: best outcomes, because inflammation is minimal.
  • Delayed repair, after 6–8 weeks: allows inflammation to subside, but needs careful interim management.
  • Intermediate repair, during active inflammation: carries the highest risk of failure.

Delayed repairs have a slightly lower success rate (70%–90%) than early repairs (85%–95%), though outcomes depend heavily on surgical expertise.

What should patients consider when choosing a surgeon?

  • Seek specialists: surgeons trained in hepatobiliary or gastrointestinal surgery.
  • Prefer high-volume centres: hospitals experienced in complex bile duct repair have lower complication rates.
  • Ask about outcomes: the surgeon's own experience and results in bile duct injury repair.
