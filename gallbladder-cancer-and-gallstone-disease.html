Gallbladder cancer and gallstone disease: the connection

Gallstones are common and gallbladder cancer is not — but the two are linked. Understanding how, and who that link actually applies to, is what separates useful vigilance from unnecessary worry.

What are gallstones, and how do they form?

Gallstones are solid particles that form in the gallbladder from components of bile such as cholesterol, bile salts and bilirubin. They develop when bile composition falls out of balance, often driven by:

  • Obesity.
  • Rapid weight loss.
  • A high-fat, low-fibre diet.
  • Genetics.

How common are gallstones in people with gallbladder cancer?

Gallstones are present in 80–90% of patients with gallbladder cancer, making it one of the most strongly associated risk factors. Only a tiny fraction of people with gallstones will ever develop gallbladder cancer.

How do gallstones increase the risk?

The mechanism is chronic inflammation rather than the stones themselves.

  • Irritation of the gallbladder wall: persistent stones cause repeated injury and inflammation of the lining.
  • Cellular damage: chronic inflammation alters the DNA of gallbladder cells, increasing the risk of malignancy.
  • Porcelain gallbladder: prolonged inflammation may deposit calcium in the wall, a state associated with higher cancer risk.

Does size or duration of gallstone disease affect risk?

  • Larger gallstones are associated with a higher risk of cancer.
  • Chronic gallstone disease: long-standing stones raise risk through prolonged inflammation and damage.

Who is at higher risk among gallstone patients?

  • Women: gallbladder cancer is more common in women, partly because gallstone disease is.
  • Older adults: risk rises with age, particularly after 60.
  • Geographic and ethnic factors: higher rates are seen in South Asia, Latin America and Indigenous populations in North America.
  • Family history: genetics play a role in both gallstone disease and gallbladder cancer.

What symptoms should people with gallstones watch for?

Gallstones often cause no symptoms at all. The signs that suggest a complication — or cancer — are:

  • Persistent upper-right abdominal pain.
  • Unexplained weight loss.
  • Jaundice.
  • A palpable lump in the abdomen.
  • Fever or chills suggesting infection.

Does removing the gallbladder reduce cancer risk?

Yes. Cholecystectomy is the most effective way to eliminate the risk of gallbladder cancer in patients with symptomatic gallstones or high-risk features such as:

  • Large gallstones.
  • Porcelain gallbladder.
  • Gallbladder polyps larger than 1 cm.

For asymptomatic gallstones, surgery is usually not recommended unless other risk factors are present.

How is cancer detected in gallstone patients?

Early detection is difficult because the symptoms overlap with benign gallstone disease. The available methods are:

  • Imaging: ultrasound, CT or MRI to detect abnormalities.
  • Endoscopic procedures: ERCP or endoscopic ultrasound for detailed evaluation and biopsy.
  • Tumour markers: CA 19-9 and CEA may assist, though neither is definitive.

Can prevention lower the risk?

  • Maintain a healthy weight.
  • Eat a balanced diet, rich in fibre and low in unhealthy fats.
  • Stay physically active.
  • Treat symptomatic gallstones promptly rather than delaying advised surgery.
